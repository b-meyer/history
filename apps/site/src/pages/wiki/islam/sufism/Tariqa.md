# The Tariqa as an Institution

The Arabic word tariqa means a road or path, and in [[sufism]] it names both the spiritual path itself and the manner of travelling along it. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] More often it is the general term for the organized Sufi brotherhoods, each formed around a master of spiritual authority or around his memory. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] The Encyclopedia of Religion says these orders share obedience to a living master, fixed rites of initiation, their own devotions and code of manners, a building other than a mosque, and funding from pious endowments (waqf). [@eor-tariqah, preamble] Several of them date their growth into formal institutions to the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. [@eor-tariqah, preamble]

## From Circle to Order

The orders grew out of the bond between a master and his pupil, which resembled the bond between teacher and student in the madrasa or between master and apprentice in the craft guilds. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] A preacher, healer or ascetic might draw listeners who came for his blessing (barakah), and some stayed as the seekers (murid) of a guide called shaykh in Arabic and pir in Persian. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] Before the twelfth century these disciples had little structure among themselves. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] The Encyclopedia's article on Sufism says that in the eleventh century a master's circle often held together only until he died and then broke up, and that by the thirteenth century many groups had become "self-perpetuating social organizations" whose authority passed from shaykh to disciple. [@eor-sufism, preamble]

The earliest known Sufi convent stood on the island of Abadan in the Persian Gulf. [@eor-sufism, preamble] The article on the tariqa credits it to the ascetic ʿAbd al-Wahid ibn Zayd, who died around 750, and says it went on after his death. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] The article on Sufism dates it to the later eighth century. [@eor-sufism, preamble]

The same article ties the firmer structure of the eleventh century to the Seljuks, Sunni rulers who took over the madrasas and gave public support to Sufi houses, which let them shape the piety taught there and kept those houses alive. [@eor-sufism, preamble] The tariqa article points to two models the Sufis had before them, the madrasa, which licensed teachers and paid students, and the young men's chivalric societies (futuwwa) that the Abbasid caliph al-Nasir encouraged in the thirteenth century. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] A master's community that outlived him by a generation came to be called his tariqa, his "method". [@eor-tariqah, preamble] Some orders' chains of masters place the start of this process in the twelfth century, but the tariqa article finds that the founders' children or grandchildren usually did the organizing, which "can be clearly documented for the late thirteenth and fourteenth centuries". [@eor-tariqah, preamble] In the [[mevlevi]], Rumi's disciples at Konya were formed into an order by his son Sultan Walad, who died in 1312. [@eor-tariqah, preamble]

The [[qadiriyya]] is commonly seen as the first order with a settled structure, and the [[chishtiyya]] as the first to arise in the Indian subcontinent. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] The [[mevlevi]] trace their chain to [[rumi]]. [@eor-sufism, preamble] The [[naqshbandiyya]] began as a Central Asian order. [@eor-islam-south-asia, preamble]

## The Chain and the Cloak

An order's authority rests on its silsila, the "chain" that links its founder through generations of masters to the Prophet, in the way an isnad vouches for a hadith report. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] The tariqa article says not all of these chains are "historically plausible". [@eor-tariqah, preamble] Most meet at Jaʿfar al-Sadiq (d. 765) and reach Muhammad through ʿAli, which gives ʿAli a special place for Sunni and Shiʿi Sufis alike. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] The Sufism article sorts the chains into two broad groups, a sober one going back to [[junayd]] and a more "intoxicated" one going back to Abu Yazid al-Bistami. [@eor-sufism, preamble] It adds that belonging to either says only where one's lineage runs, not how one regards mystical experience. [@eor-sufism, preamble] A shaykh's chain gave him legitimacy as the hadith chain does a report, since through it each order could claim to stand on the foundations of Sufism. [@eor-sufism, preamble]

When a master's method and circle survived him, his authority passed with a ceremonial patched cloak, the khirqa, to one or more chosen disciples. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] The successor might be one of the shaykh's sons, a disciple he named, or a man the community elected. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] A new disciple swore allegiance both to his own shaykh and to the founder of the line, and so gained the order's own dhikr formulas and a share in the power of the whole chain. [@eor-tariqah, preamble]

The chain also raised the master's standing. [@eor-sufism, preamble] A shaykh's lineage was taken to mean that the spiritual power of every master in it had reached him, and the heads of the great orders came to be called awliyaʾ, friends of God. [@eor-sufism, preamble] Some were hailed by their followers as the qutb, the "pole" or "axis" of the cosmos, and rival claims to that rank caused friction between the orders. [@eor-sufism, preamble] Their tombs became places of pilgrimage. [@eor-sufism, preamble]

Most orders required a living guide. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] The Uwaysis, named after Uways al-Qarani, said they had been initiated through a dream or vision of a past master instead. [@eor-tariqah, preamble]

## The Lodge

A growing circle might move from the master's house or shop into a compound with a hall for devotions, a large kitchen, a small mosque and sometimes a school, and larger centres housed initiates in cells or dormitories. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] Many grew up around the founder's tomb or a local shrine. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] The names differed by region, with zawiya and ribat in the Maghreb, tekke in Anatolia and the Balkans, khanaqah in Iran and India and, as khanqah, in Egypt and the Levant, and dargah, especially in India. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] The Sufism article says that by the thirteenth century the ribat was a residence or training centre from the Arab lands, the khanqah a similar house from Khorasan, and the zawiya the place of more serious training under a teaching shaykh. [@eor-sufism, preamble] The Chishti shaykhs of India kept to their own homes, called jamaʿat-khana, so that disciples would not be caught up in running a large centre and its endowments. [@eor-tariqah, preamble]

The shaykh lived in one part of the lodge with his family, met his disciples at set hours and led the daily prayers. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] The Saʿid al-Suʿadaʾ in Egypt, founded by Saladin in 1173, housed three hundred dervishes. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] The lodges took in travellers, treated the sick and helped the poor. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] The orders also enrolled lay associates who kept their trades, prayed in the order's setting under the shaykh and joined its dhikr and samaʿ. [@eor-tariqah, preamble]

Endowments in land and income paid for the lodges and shrines. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] In Seljuk Anatolia, inscriptions show women of ruling families paying for the building of Sufi lodges. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] In South Asia rulers gave shaykhs pensions and tax-free lands, and most orders accepted them. [@eor-islam-south-asia, preamble] There the tomb-shrine (dargah) "began to supplant" the hospice in popular devotion. [@eor-islam-south-asia, preamble]

## Initiation

The tariqa article gives a model initiation from a manual of the [[qadiriyya]]. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] The candidate washes, prays two rakʿas and sits facing the shaykh, clasping his right hand, and recites the opening sura of the Qurʾan and blessings on the Prophet and on the chains through which the shaykh holds his authority. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] He then repeats after the shaykh a formula that asks God's forgiveness, accepts the shaykh's hand as that of [[abd-al-qadir-al-jilani|ʿAbd al-Qadir]], and promises to recite the dhikr as the shaykh directs. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] The shaykh closes with a prayer and the Qurʾanic verse of allegiance (48:10). [@eor-tariqah, preamble]

In the Sufism article, the shaykh initiated full members and gave them the patched frock, and they were urged to yield to his will "like dead bodies in the hands of the body-washers". [@eor-sufism, preamble]

## Dhikr and Samaʿ

Dhikr, the "remembrance" of God, is the rhythmic repetition of words or phrases, usually names of God from the Qurʾan or forms of the profession of faith, often with controlled breathing. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] In some orders, the [[naqshbandiyya]] among them, dhikr is private and moves from the voice toward silence. [@eor-sufism, preamble]

Samaʿ was a listening session at which poetry was sung to music and listeners might answer with rhythmic movement. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] There were samaʿ houses in [[baghdad]] as early as 850. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] Most of the ʿulamaʾ rejected it, and not every order accepted it, though many Sufi manuals defend it under rules. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] Many shaykhs who allowed it let only seasoned Sufis take part and warned novices away. [@eor-sufism, preamble] The Sufism article says these rites brought Sufism within reach of Muslims for whom the teaching of [[ibn-arabi]] and his followers was too abstruse. [@eor-sufism, preamble]

## Manners and Etiquette

One of the earliest manuals of conduct for members of an order is Abu al-Najib al-Suhrawardi's Adab al-muridin, "The manners of the disciples", from the twelfth century. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] It places the Sufis beside the traditionists and the jurists and has them rely on both. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] Much of it governs daily conduct, from cleanliness, plain dress and restraint at meals to how a shaykh should treat his guests and they him. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] The Encyclopedia reads the book as showing that the orders did not regard themselves as sects apart from the ordinary religious disciplines. [@eor-tariqah, preamble]

## Women in the Orders

The orders worked in public, which in most Muslim societies was men's space, and the person on the path was assumed to be a man. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] Al-Sulami (d. 1022) compiled lives of Sufi women besides Rabiʿa, and [[ibn-arabi]] wrote of Sufi women, one of them his teacher. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] Women most often heard talks at the lodges from a separate gallery or behind a curtain or grille. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] The tariqa article finds women's public part in the orders poorly recorded for the medieval period and takes open, mixed participation to be a development of the late twentieth century. [@eor-tariqah, preamble]

## Spread and Patrons

From the twelfth and thirteenth centuries the orders spread across the Middle East, Africa, Arabia, Central, South and Southeast Asia and China. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] After the Mongols took Baghdad in 1258 they kept up contact between the Arabic, Turkish and Persian lands, and under the Mughal and Ottoman empires they either joined the ruling classes or became part of the new societies. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] In South Asia the Mughal emperors endowed the Chishti shrine at [[ajmer]], and Akbar made fourteen pilgrimages to it. [@eor-islam-south-asia, preamble] The Encyclopedia's South Asia article doubts that the Sufi orders of medieval India stood as a religious establishment apart from the political ones. [@eor-islam-south-asia, preamble]

## Suhrawardiyya

The [[suhrawardiyya]] is one of the oldest orders. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] The tariqa article names Abu al-Najib al-Suhrawardi (d. 1168), professor of Shafiʿi law at the Nizamiyya college in [[baghdad]] and author of the Adab al-muridin, as founder, and says his nephew and student Shihab al-Din Abu Hafs ʿUmar al-Suhrawardi (1145 to 1234) gave it its decisive form. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] The Sufism article starts the order's genealogy with Abu al-Najib but says the fraternity bearing the name was founded by Shihab al-Din. [@eor-sufism, preamble] The caliph al-Nasir built a ribat for Shihab al-Din and his disciples in 1203. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] His disciples spread the order through Asia Minor, Syria, Persia and northern India, and after the fourteenth century it split into many branches. [@eor-tariqah, preamble]

In South Asia the Suhrawardiyya and the Chishtiyya were the two main Sufi groups of the sultanate period, opposed in their rites and practices. [@eor-islam-south-asia, preamble] The Suhrawardi headquarters were at [[multan]], home of Bahaʾ al-Din Zakariya, and at [[uchch]], home of Jalal al-Din Surkhpush Bukhari (d. 1292) and his grandson Makhdum-i Jahaniyan (1308 to 1384). [@eor-islam-south-asia, preamble] The South Asia article gives Bahaʾ al-Din's dates as 1182 to 1262, and the Sufism article has him die in 1268. [@eor-sufism, preamble] The order had great influence at the court of thirteenth-century Delhi. [@eor-sufism, preamble] In the Punjab it has always had state patronage. [@eor-islam-south-asia, preamble]

## Shadhiliyya

Abu al-Hasan al-Shadhili began as a preacher at [[tunis]] and founded the [[shadhiliyya]] there in 1227. [@eor-sufism, preamble] He travelled widely in the Maghreb and Spain and settled at last in [[alexandria]], where he died, and the tariqa article gives his dates as 1196 to 1258. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] In the Sufism article he moved east after a vision and settled in Egypt, where the order flourished. [@eor-sufism, preamble] Its devotions are inward and silent, and al-Shadhili taught his followers to seek God through their daily duties instead of in solitude or in a lodge. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] Unlike orders that asked members to leave their trade or family, the Shadhiliyya let them stay in the world. [@eor-sufism, preamble] Its best-known early shaykh is its third head, Ibn ʿAtaʾ Allah of Alexandria (d. 1309), whose Hikam, a book of maxims, is still widely read. [@eor-sufism, preamble]

## Safaviyya

The [[safaviyya]] traced its lineage to Safi al-Din of [[ardabil]] (1252 to 1334), a Sunni Sufi teacher. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] His descendants turned the order to Shiʿism, made it a militant movement and conquered Iran at the end of the fifteenth century, founding the Safavid dynasty. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] The article on Shiʿism says that in the fifteenth century the Kizilbash Türkmen federation and religious order took up an extreme Shiʿi doctrine under Safavid leadership, and once the Safavid state was founded its rulers made Twelver Shiʿism the official religion. [@eor-shiism-overview, preamble]
