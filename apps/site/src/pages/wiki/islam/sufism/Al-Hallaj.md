# al-Hallaj

Al-Husayn ibn Mansur al-Hallaj (857 to 922) was a mystic of [[sufism]] whom Muslims came to call "the martyr of mystical love". [@eor-hallaj, preamble] His name means "the cotton carder". [@eor-hallaj, preamble] The Encyclopedia of Religion calls him "the most famous of the Baghdad mystics". [@eor-sufism, preamble]

## Life

He was born in 857 at al-Tur, in the Iranian province of Fars. [@eor-sufism, preamble] His initiation into Sufism began while he was still a teenager. [@eor-sufism, preamble] Al-Hujwiri, writing in the eleventh century, calls him "a Persian and a native of Bayḍá". [@hujwiri-kashf-al-mahjub-nicholson, §11.53]

### Teachers and Junayd

As a young man he spent time with Sahl al-Tustari, whose theory of the light of Muhammad later shaped his own thought. [@eor-hallaj, preamble] The Encyclopedia of Religion's entry on him says he went on from Basra to [[baghdad]], then the centre of mystical learning, and "frequented such Ṣūfī masters as al-Junayd". [@eor-hallaj, preamble] Its entry on Sufism names Sahl al-Tustari, ʿAmr al-Makki and [[junayd]] among the masters who trained him over more than twenty years. [@eor-sufism, preamble] It calls Junayd "his former teacher", who later ostracized him. [@eor-sufism, preamble]

[[al-hujwiri]] tells it differently in the [[kashf-al-mahjub]]. In his account al-Hallaj studied first under Sahl and then under ʿAmr al-Makki, leaving each without permission, and then "sought to associate with Junayd, but Junayd would not receive him". [@hujwiri-kashf-al-mahjub-nicholson, §11.53] Al-Hujwiri gives this as the reason all the shaykhs banned him. [@hujwiri-kashf-al-mahjub-nicholson, §11.53]

A legend places the break with Junayd after al-Hallaj's first pilgrimage to Mecca. When he knocked at Junayd's door and was asked who was there, he is said to have answered "Anā al-ḥaqq", "I am the Creative Truth". [@eor-hallaj, preamble] The entry says the truth of this story cannot be established, though the phrase appears in his Kitab al-tawasin. [@eor-hallaj, preamble]

### Wandering and Preaching

After the clash with Junayd, or for some other reason, he gave up the Sufi robe and travelled through Iran and Khorasan, then made a second pilgrimage with four hundred disciples. [@eor-hallaj, preamble] He went on to India, by his adversaries' account "to learn magic", by his own "to call the people to God". [@eor-hallaj, preamble] From Gujarat he passed through Sind and the Punjab and reached Turfan. [@eor-hallaj, preamble] People came to call him ḥallāj al-asrār, "the carder of consciences", a play on his family name. [@eor-sufism, preamble]

The Encyclopedia of Religion describes his preaching as a call to moral reform and to the experience of union with the Beloved. [@eor-sufism, preamble] It was told that in the mosques of Baghdad he would call people to God and then beg them to kill him. [@eor-hallaj, preamble]

### Prison and Execution

On his return to Baghdad both the orthodox and the Sufis met him with more hostility than before. [@eor-hallaj, preamble] Arrested on the road to Sus during a third pilgrimage, he was pilloried and in 913 imprisoned in Baghdad. [@eor-hallaj, preamble] The chamberlain Nasr al-Qashuri and the mother of the young caliph al-Muqtadir protected him in prison. [@eor-hallaj, preamble] He spent about eight years there. [@eor-sufism, preamble]

In March 922 the government drew up a death sentence against him. [@eor-hallaj, preamble] His disciples recorded that he was flogged, mutilated, exposed on a gibbet and beheaded, and that his body was burned. [@eor-sufism, preamble] He died on 26 March 922 (24 Dhu al-Qaʿda 309). [@eor-hallaj, preamble]

## Why He Was Executed

The Sufi tradition explains his death as punishment for ifshāʾ al-sirr, "divulging the secret". [@eor-hallaj, preamble] The Encyclopedia of Religion's entry on Sufism says that by speaking publicly of mystical union he broke a cardinal rule of the masters of his generation, that such experience was for fellow adepts only. [@eor-sufism, preamble] To non-Sufis and to sober Sufis, his cry "Anā al-Ḥāqq!" sounded like shirk or like the Christian doctrine of incarnation. [@eor-sufism, preamble]

The Encyclopedia of Religion's entry on Sufism holds that his execution came as much from politics as from mysticism. [@eor-sufism, preamble] Louis Massignon showed how unsettled Baghdad was in those decades, with powerless caliphs, viziers who changed often, and fear of the Qaramita in Bahrain. [@eor-hallaj, preamble] The authorities asked whether al-Hallaj had dealings with the Qaramita, and he had spoken publicly of the isqāṭ al-farāʾiḍ, substitutes for personal duties such as the fast or the pilgrimage. [@eor-hallaj, preamble] One vizier defended him and the next condemned him, and the caliph whose mother had protected him finally sentenced him. [@eor-sufism, preamble]

## Teaching and Writings

The only work of his preserved complete is the Kitab al-tawasin, probably written in prison. [@eor-hallaj, preamble] In it he brought into Sufism the parable of the moth that burns itself in the candle flame, a figure for the lover. [@eor-hallaj, preamble] The same book makes Iblis and Pharaoh his models, since both were condemned by God yet held to their course. [@eor-sufism, preamble] His poem "Kill me, my trusted friends" asks for death as the way to life. [@eor-sufism, preamble]

Later Persianate poetry made him the chief voice of waḥdat al-wujūd, the unity of being, but Massignon argued that he held instead to waḥdat al-shuhūd, the unity of witness. [@eor-hallaj, preamble] The Encyclopedia of Religion's entry on Sufism doubts he meant to be a metaphysician at all, and puts the charges against him down to misreading of his ecstatic language. [@eor-sufism, preamble]

## Al-Hujwiri's Judgement

Al-Hujwiri calls him "an enamoured and intoxicated votary of Ṣúfiism" and reports that the Sufi shaykhs disagreed about him. [@hujwiri-kashf-al-mahjub-nicholson, §11.53] He lists ʿAmr al-Makki, Ibn Khafif and others among those who accepted him, and Junayd, Shibli and others among those who suspended judgement. [@hujwiri-kashf-al-mahjub-nicholson, §11.53] He quotes [[al-qushayri]] as leaving the matter to God. [@hujwiri-kashf-al-mahjub-nicholson, §11.53] He says he saw fifty of al-Hallaj's works in Baghdad and its neighbourhood and in Khuzistan, Fars and Khurasan. [@hujwiri-kashf-al-mahjub-nicholson, §11.53] He rejects the charge of magic. [@hujwiri-kashf-al-mahjub-nicholson, §11.53] Yet he warns that al-Hallaj's sayings are no model, because he was an ecstatic and not "firmly settled". [@hujwiri-kashf-al-mahjub-nicholson, §11.53]

## Later Memory

Ibn Khafif, his last visitor in prison, wrote down some of his sayings. [@eor-hallaj, preamble] His revival began in the twelfth century, and ʿAttar's chapter on him in the Tadhkirat al-awliyaʾ became the source for almost every later telling in the Persianate world. [@eor-hallaj, preamble] [[rumi]] quoted his verses, though he held his own master Shams al-Din far above him. [@eor-hallaj, preamble] The sober orders stayed critical, and Ibn Taymiyya led orthodox dislike of him. [@eor-hallaj, preamble]
