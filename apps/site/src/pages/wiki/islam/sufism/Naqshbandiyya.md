# Naqshbandiyya

The Naqshbandiyya is a [[sufism|Sufi]] order of Central Asian origin. [@eor-islam-south-asia, preamble] [[baha-al-din-naqshband]] formulated its path, and from Central Asia the order spread to India and China and to the Ottoman Empire. [@eor-islam-central-asia, preamble] What set it apart from the Yasavi order, its Central Asian neighbour, was a silent or hidden dhikr where the Yasavis chanted theirs aloud. [@eor-islam-central-asia, preamble]

## Baha al-Din Naqshband

Baha al-Din Naqshband was born in 1318. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] The Encyclopedia of Religion gives his death as 1388 in its entry on the Sufi orders. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] Its entry on Islam in Central Asia gives 1389. [@eor-islam-central-asia, preamble]

He traced his spiritual descent through Amir Kulal, who advised Timur on spiritual matters, back to the Khwajagan, a Persian-speaking line of Sufis in Central Asia. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] The Central Asia entry places him at the end of one of two chains of authority that went back to Yusuf Hamadani (1048 to 1141), who had founded a Sufi monastery at [[merv|Merv]]. [@eor-islam-central-asia, preamble] The other chain led to Ahmad Yasavi and the Yasavi order. [@eor-islam-central-asia, preamble]

Baha al-Din founded the order in [[bukhara]] and left the city only three times. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] Twice he went on pilgrimage to Mecca, and once he travelled to meet Muʿizz al-Din Husayn, the ruler of [[herat]], and taught him the Naqshbandi principles. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] His tomb stands in a large shrine complex and draws pilgrims. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] He soon tied his order to the trade and craft guilds and to the merchant houses, and his wealth rose with his spiritual influence. [@eor-tariqah, preamble]

## Central Asia and the Timurid Court

The Central Asia entry calls the Naqshbandiyya the most politically influential order in Central Asia. [@eor-islam-central-asia, preamble] It traces the order's hold on state affairs back to the Mongol period, when the order helped bring the conquerors to Islam. [@eor-islam-central-asia, preamble] The Mongol khans ruled Transoxiana and also led the steppe tribes, so the order gained a favoured place among both the settled people and the nomads. [@eor-islam-central-asia, preamble] Its position grew stronger under Timur, who may have been a disciple of one of Baha al-Din's teachers. [@eor-islam-central-asia, preamble] The order appealed mostly to the settled, Iranian-speaking population, while the Yasavis did best among Turkic-speaking nomads, but the Naqshbandis also had many nomad followers. [@eor-islam-central-asia, preamble]

The entry on the orders says the Naqshbandiyya soon won power at the Timurid court and oversaw the administration of religious law. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] It names Khwajah Ahrar "of Herat" (1404 to 1490) as the leader under whom the order "virtually dominated political life in Central Asia". [@eor-tariqah, preamble] Ahrar held that "to serve the world, it is necessary to exercise political power", meaning that rulers had to be kept in check so that they applied the divine law. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] The Central Asia entry gives him the same dates and describes him as a magnate who owned vast farmland and urban settlements, without naming Herat. [@eor-islam-central-asia, preamble]

For most of the sixteenth century Naqshbandi shaykhs made and unmade khans in Central Asia by setting rival claimants against each other. [@eor-islam-central-asia, preamble] Their influence fell away in the seventeenth century, above all under the Manghit dynasty, which took the throne in 1753. [@eor-islam-central-asia, preamble]

From Bukhara the order spread until it rivalled the Qadiriyya in following, with branches in Afghanistan, Iraq, Turkey, China and the Indonesian islands. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] Its literature is in Persian, and the poet ʿAbd al-Rahman Jami (d. 1492) was closely involved in it. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] In Persia the Shiʿi Safavid dynasty uprooted the order in the sixteenth century because of its Sunni loyalties. [@eor-tariqah, preamble]

## Silent Dhikr

Unlike the Chishtiyya, the Naqshbandis performed their dhikr in silence and forbade music and rhythmic movement. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] They believed that wordless dhikr could bring the seeker to a contemplation in which subject and object can no longer be told apart and the soul returns to God as it was before creation. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] Their meditation included concentrating on their shaykh and visiting the tombs of saints to draw strength from the spirit of the dead master. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] The Encyclopedia of Religion's entry on Sufism names the Naqshbandiyya among the groups for whom dhikr is a private exercise. [@eor-sufism, preamble]

The order asked for no heroic austerities. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] It judged purifying the heart more useful than hard mortification of the lower soul, and it taught that the safest course lay between too much hunger and too much eating. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] Poets took to this sober method, and by the early eighteenth century every leading poet in the Indo-Persian style was a member of the order or under its influence. [@eor-tariqah, preamble]

## India

The South Asia entry credits Khwajah Muhammad al-Baqi Billah (1563/4 to 1603) with bringing the order to the subcontinent. [@eor-islam-south-asia, preamble] It became prominent there from the seventeenth century, when its members began to challenge the established forms of Sufism. [@eor-islam-south-asia, preamble]

In Mughal India the order led a reaction against the Din-i ilahi ("Divine Religion"), the syncretist faith of the emperor Akbar. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] It stayed involved in politics until 1740, including a strong reaction against Hindu practices. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] The entry on the orders gives "the Naqshbandī-inspired reform movement in seventeenth-century Delhi" as an example of the orders' work for the observance of Islamic law in India. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] In the eighteenth century the Naqshbandi Shah Wali Allah (1703 to 1762), who was also a member of the Qadiriyya, became the greatest reformer of [[delhi]]. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] The South Asia entry says he tried to reconcile the rival mystical philosophies of waḥdat al-wujūd ("unity of existence") and waḥdat ash-shuhūd ("unity of vision"). [@eor-islam-south-asia, preamble]

In Sind a reform movement begun by Naqshbandis led poets to teach the basic duties of Islam in simple verse. [@eor-islam-south-asia, preamble] One of them, Makhdum Muhammad Hashim (d. 1761), wrote several works explaining the essentials of Islam in Sindhi verse. [@eor-islam-south-asia, preamble]

## Ahmad Sirhindi

[[ahmad-sirhindi]] (1564 to 1624) was an Indian Sufi and a prolific writer on mysticism and theology. [@eor-sirhindi, preamble] Khwajah Baqi Billah initiated him into the order in 1600. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] The South Asia entry places the initiation in Baqi Billah's last years and calls Sirhindi "the most influential member of the order in South Asia". [@eor-islam-south-asia, preamble] Within the order he gathered many disciples, taught them its mystical doctrine, and widened its influence in India and elsewhere. [@eor-sirhindi, preamble] His collected letters, written to fellow Sufis and to some state officials, were often praised as a landmark of Muslim religious thought in India. [@eor-sirhindi, preamble]

He was known as the mujaddid-i alf-i thani, the "renewer of the second millennium" of the Islamic era. [@eor-sirhindi, preamble] He described a change in the structure of the mystical realities at the end of the first millennium, after which the prophetic perfections that had faded since Muhammad's death shone again in the renewer. [@eor-sirhindi, preamble] The Sirhindi entry thinks he probably saw himself in that role and says his disciples certainly did. [@eor-sirhindi, preamble] The South Asia entry adds that he called himself the qayyum, a mediator through whom all benefits passed between God and humankind. [@eor-islam-south-asia, preamble] His letters to the emperor Jahangir and the Mughal nobles about practices he judged un-Islamic earned him a short time in prison, and Jahangir called them a "bunch of absurdities". [@eor-islam-south-asia, preamble]

His part in Mughal religious politics is disputed. [@eor-sirhindi, preamble] The entry on the orders names him as an important figure in the reaction against Akbar's Din-i ilahi. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] The Sirhindi entry reports that he strongly opposed Akbar's conciliatory policy toward the Hindus and that many scholars credit him with turning back the heretical trends of Akbar's reign. [@eor-sirhindi, preamble] The same entry cites recent research that finds this view far from certain, since nothing shows that the empire changed its attitude to the Hindus because of him. [@eor-sirhindi, preamble] The entry itself describes him as first and foremost a seeker after religious truth and says that the great majority of his letters deal with typically Sufi issues. [@eor-sirhindi, preamble]

Those questions were prophecy and sainthood, how the religious law relates to the mystical path, and two competing accounts of unity, "unity of being (waḥdat al-wujūd)" and "unity of appearance (waḥdat al-shuhūd )". [@eor-sirhindi, preamble] The entry places him in the line of [[ibn-arabi]] on these matters, saying he "belongs to the stream of mystical thought established by Ibn al-ʿArabī, though they differ in certain aspects". [@eor-sirhindi, preamble]
