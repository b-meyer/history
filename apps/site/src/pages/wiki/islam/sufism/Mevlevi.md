# Mevlevi

The Mevlevi are a Sufi order, a branch of [[sufism]] that traces its chain of masters to [[rumi]]. [@eor-sufism, preamble] The name is the Turkish adjectival form of Mawlawi, "my master", a title by which Rumi was addressed. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] In the West its members are called the Whirling Dervishes. [@eor-rumi, preamble]

## Konya Before the Order

Rumi's father, Baha al-Din Walad, who died in 1231, held a madrasa at [[konya]] from the Saljuq sultan, and it worked more as a Sufi centre than as a college of law. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] The Encyclopedia of Religion says the hagiographies exaggerate his place as the seminal figure of the order. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] A disciple of his, Burhan al-Din, reached Konya in the early 1230s and brought Rumi into the mystical life. [@eor-rumi, preamble] Rumi later led the Konya disciples and gathered a following of his own. [@eor-tariqah, preamble]

## Who Founded the Order

The founding is disputed. Redhouse's introduction to his translation of the Mevlevi lives says Rumi "is said to have instituted his peculiar order of dervishes", with its dress and its music. [@rumi-mesnevi-redhouse, preamble] One of the anecdotes Redhouse translates, set in Sultan Veled's time, has the Mevlevi dervishes wearing a turban end "in imitation of their founder, Jelāl". [@rumi-mesnevi-redhouse, §3.76] The Encyclopedia of Religion disagrees and says Rumi set up no formal order in his lifetime and that his son Sultan Walad organized it. [@eor-sufism, preamble] Its entry on the orders says Sultan Walad, who died in 1312, turned Rumi's community of disciples in Konya into an order. [@eor-tariqah, preamble]

Rumi died at Konya on 17 December 1273. [@eor-rumi, preamble] His first successor, Husam al-Din, died in 1284, and Sultan Walad then took over the disciples and made them into a Sufi fraternity. [@eor-rumi, preamble] He fixed the form of the samāʿ, the mystical dance, and by his own death in 1312 the order was firmly established. [@eor-rumi, preamble] The order's beginning therefore falls between Rumi's death in 1273 and Sultan Walad's in 1312. [@eor-rumi, preamble]

Sultan Walad built a shrine dome, a türbe, over Rumi's grave at Konya. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] Another anecdote in Redhouse tells of a great merchant who came to Konya in Sultan Veled's day to visit Rumi's tomb. [@rumi-mesnevi-redhouse, §3.77]

## The Mathnawi and the Ceremony

The order takes its identity from Rumi's [[mathnawi]] and his Divan-i Shams-i Tabrizi. [@eor-sufism, preamble] D'Herbelot, quoted at the head of Redhouse's introduction, writes that the Mevlevis "font leur Capital de l’ouvrage de leur Maistre" and respect it almost as much as the Quran, so that the Mathnawi is often called the Mevlevi too. [@rumi-mesnevi-redhouse, preamble]

The order is best known for its samāʿ, which joins music and poetry to a whirling dance. [@eor-sufism, preamble] An initiate served 1001 days in the kitchen of the lodge before taking part in the turning ceremony, a spinning done in robes and hats to an ensemble that nearly always had drums and a reed flute. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] Where the Bektashiyah drew the less literate, the Mevlevi drew a more educated elite. [@eor-sufism, preamble]

## Lodges in the Ottoman Centuries

From Konya, Sultan Walad and his son Ulu Arif Chalabi set up Mevlevi lodges across Anatolia, each under a deputy shaykh. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] The order worked mostly inside the Ottoman Empire, where it grew rich and close to the imperial court. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] Leadership was hereditary, and the central organization kept the order from splitting. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] In the mid-sixteenth century Diwanah Muhammad Chalabi and Yusuf Sinachak brought [[shia|Shii]] influences into the order and changed its character somewhat. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] The order promoted calligraphy and Persian literature, though its members lived almost entirely among speakers of Turkish or Arabic. [@eor-tariqah, preamble]

The Encyclopedia of Religion names no lodge city and gives no year for the order between 1312 and the late eighteenth century, apart from the sixteenth-century change, so the dated record for those centuries is thin. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] From the late eighteenth century, European visitors to [[constantinople|Istanbul]] made a point of watching the turning ceremony at a Mevlevi lodge. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] By the twentieth century the Bektashiyah and the Khalwatiyah, whose membership overlapped with the Mevlevi, seemed more active than the Mevlevi. [@eor-tariqah, preamble]

The order kept a strong hold on Turkish music and poetry. [@eor-rumi, preamble] Kemal Atatürk abolished it in 1925 along with every other mystical fraternity. [@eor-rumi, preamble]
