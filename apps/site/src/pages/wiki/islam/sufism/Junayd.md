# Junayd and the Baghdad School

Abu al-Qasim al-Junayd, who died in AH 298 (910 CE), was a leading figure of the Baghdad school of [[sufism]], and the Encyclopedia of Religion ties him to its "sober" and socially responsible side. [@eor-junayd, preamble] Later Sufis set his path of sobriety against the path of intoxication they linked with Abu Yazid al-Bistami. [@eor-sufism, preamble]

## Life

Al-Junayd's family were Iranian merchants. His father sold glassware, and he made his own living as a silk dealer. [@eor-junayd, preamble] His paternal uncle Sari al-Saqati, often counted among the doyens of Baghdad Sufism, drew him to the mystical and ascetic life, and in time al-Junayd took his uncle's place as leader of the Baghdad school. [@eor-junayd, preamble]

He trained in law and theology under Shafiʿi scholars such as Abu Thawr and Ibn Kullab, both of whom died around 855, and he was qualified to give legal opinions. [@eor-junayd, preamble] Most of his teachers belonged to Sufi circles, and he held long talks on mystical experience and the pious life with the Baghdad ascetic al-Harith al-Muhasibi, who died in 857. [@eor-junayd, preamble] His epistles and sayings show the mark of al-Muhasibi's mystical psychology. [@eor-junayd, preamble]

In the [[kashf-al-mahjub]], [[al-hujwiri]] says that Junayd's mother was Sari's sister and that Junayd was Sari's disciple. [@hujwiri-kashf-al-mahjub-nicholson, §11.31] He tells that Junayd would not preach to his own disciples while Sari lived, and turned down the pleas of the shaykhs of [[baghdad]], until the Prophet told him in a dream to speak. [@hujwiri-kashf-al-mahjub-nicholson, §11.31] Al-Hujwiri adds that "all Ṣúfís unanimously acknowledge his leadership". [@hujwiri-kashf-al-mahjub-nicholson, §11.31]

His public sermons drew high state officials and theologians as well as fellow mystics. [@eor-junayd, preamble] Among his associates and disciples were Abu Saʿid al-Kharraz, Abu al-Husayn al-Nuri and Abu Bakr al-Shibli. [@eor-junayd, preamble] When he died, his disciple al-Jurayri followed him as head of the Baghdad school. [@eor-junayd, preamble]

## Sobriety and Intoxication

Later Sufi tradition made al-Junayd the chief voice of "sober" mysticism and set him against an "intoxicated" line that included Abu Yazid al-Bistami, al-Nuri, al-Shibli and [[al-hallaj]]. [@eor-junayd, preamble] In his comments on Abu Yazid's ecstatic utterances (shatahat) he took the side of sobriety. [@eor-junayd, preamble] The split between sober and intoxicated Sufis stayed important through the later history of Islamic mysticism. [@eor-sufism, preamble] Of the two main chains of spiritual descent behind the later orders, the more sober runs back to al-Junayd and the other to Abu Yazid, though membership in either marks a spiritual genealogy rather than a member's own stance. [@eor-sufism, preamble]

Al-Hujwiri sorts the Sufis into sects and gives Abu Yazid's followers, the Tayfuris, and Junayd's followers, the Junaydis, opposite views on the question. [@hujwiri-kashf-al-mahjub-nicholson, §14.3] He reports that the Junaydis call intoxication evil because it upsets a person's normal state and takes away sanity and self-control. [@hujwiri-kashf-al-mahjub-nicholson, §14.3] He sides with them and quotes his own shaykh, a follower of Junayd's doctrine, who called intoxication "the playground of children" and sobriety "the death-field of men". [@hujwiri-kashf-al-mahjub-nicholson, §14.3] Nicholson's preface notes that al-Hujwiri and his spiritual director al-Khuttali both took up Junayd's view that sobriety is better than intoxication. [@hujwiri-kashf-al-mahjub-nicholson, preamble]

In his chapter on the Junaydis, al-Hujwiri says that in his own time Junayd was called the "Peacock of the Divines" and that his doctrine of sobriety is the best known of all, adopted by every shaykh. [@hujwiri-kashf-al-mahjub-nicholson, §14.4] He passes on a story from the Anecdotes in which al-Hallaj leaves ʿAmr al-Makki, comes to Junayd and is turned away with the words "I do not associate with madmen". [@hujwiri-kashf-al-mahjub-nicholson, §14.4] The sources do not agree on the two men's relation. The Encyclopedia of Religion's article on Sufism names al-Junayd among the masters who trained al-Hallaj, and calls him the teacher al-Hallaj later broke away from. [@eor-sufism, preamble]

## Teaching

Al-Junayd was the first to argue clearly that all things, having come from God, must return to rest in him after their dispersion (tafriqa), a return he called jamʿ. [@eor-junayd, preamble] The seeker passes away (fanaʾ) in an encounter with the divine reality and then subsists in God (baqaʾ). [@eor-junayd, preamble] Even in baqaʾ, he taught, the mystic stays separated and veiled from God, and he used the image of a lover longing for the divine Beloved to convey that separation. [@eor-junayd, preamble] He took care not to describe the meeting of man and God as a union of two essences (ittihad). [@eor-junayd, preamble]

His writings are epistles (rasaʾil) to his contemporaries and short treatises that comment on chosen Qur'anic passages. [@eor-junayd, preamble] Their language is subtle and hard to follow, and the Encyclopedia of Religion suggests this may have been meant to keep his ideas out of reach of scholars who read only the outward sense. [@eor-junayd, preamble] Al-Hallaj imitated that style but wrote far more openly about his own experiences. [@eor-junayd, preamble]

Al-Junayd told his disciples again and again not to challenge the political and religious authorities of the day, and this conformism kept him clear of the persecutions of "heretics" in his time. [@eor-junayd, preamble]
