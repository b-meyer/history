# Ibn al-Arabi

Ibn al-Arabi (1165 to 1240) was a mystic of Muslim Spain, known across the Islamic world as al-Shaykh al-akbar, the "greatest master". [@eor-ibn-al-arabi, preamble] He wrote at least 300 works, and about 110 survive in manuscripts that can be verified, some 18 of them in his own hand. [@eor-ibn-al-arabi, preamble] He is among the best-known Sufis of al-Andalus, and like many of them he lived much of his life outside it. [@eor-islam-andalusia, preamble]

## Andalusia and North Africa

He was born at [[murcia]] on 28 July 1165. [@eor-ibn-al-arabi, preamble] He grew up in [[seville]], then the Almohad provincial capital, from the age of seven, and his father was a professional soldier in the sultan's service. [@eor-ibn-al-arabi, preamble] The Encyclopedia of Religion's article on Sufism says the family moved to Seville while he was a child and that he had most of his schooling in the traditional religious sciences there. [@eor-sufism, preamble]

At about fifteen or sixteen he went alone on retreat outside Seville, probably among the ruins of Roman Italica. [@eor-ibn-al-arabi, preamble] By his own account he saw Jesus, Moses and Muhammad there in a dream-vision, and each gave him advice. [@eor-ibn-al-arabi, preamble] Muhammad told him "Hold fast to me and you will be safe", and Ibn al-Arabi says this is why he began to study hadith. [@eor-ibn-al-arabi, preamble]

The vision led the aged philosopher [[ibn-rushd]] to ask to meet him at [[cordoba]]. [@eor-ibn-al-arabi, preamble] Asked whether mystical illumination reaches the same results as rational inquiry, Ibn al-Arabi answered "Yes and no". [@eor-ibn-al-arabi, preamble] The article on Sufism says he travelled widely in Spain and North Africa in his early twenties, describes the Cordoba meeting in the same passage, and reads it as a symbol, with Ibn Rushd standing for the philosophers' reliance on reason and Ibn al-Arabi for gnosis (maʿrifah). [@eor-sufism, preamble]

Within two years he gave up the military career his father had wanted for him and handed everything he owned to his father. [@eor-ibn-al-arabi, preamble] He sought out Sufi masters, men and women, in Seville, Cordoba and other cities of Andalusia and North Africa, and described them in his book The Spirit of Holiness. [@eor-ibn-al-arabi, preamble] The article on Sufism names two women Sufis, above all Fatimah of Cordova, as strong influences on his spiritual growth. [@eor-sufism, preamble] It also reports his claim that Khidr, a mythic figure whom Sufis honour as a saint and guide, initiated him into Sufism. [@eor-sufism, preamble]

After a retreat of at least nine months he had a vision at Cordoba in 1190 of all the prophets, from Adam to Muhammad. [@eor-ibn-al-arabi, preamble] His first journey outside Andalusia took him to [[tunis]], where he stayed in 1194 with ʿAbd al-ʿAziz al-Mahdawi, the friend and mentor the Encyclopedia of Religion says was perhaps the most important influence on him. [@eor-ibn-al-arabi, preamble] Around 1195 he took his two sisters to Fez to be married, and he spent two more years there. [@eor-ibn-al-arabi, preamble] In 1198 he went back to the Iberian Peninsula for the last time and attended Ibn Rushd's funeral at Cordoba. [@eor-ibn-al-arabi, preamble] The article on Sufism dates his final departure from Spain and North Africa to 1201. [@eor-sufism, preamble]

## Mecca and the East

After six more months with al-Mahdawi in Tunis he made a tour of the pilgrimage sites at Hebron, Jerusalem and Medina, and he reached [[mecca]] in mid-1202. [@eor-ibn-al-arabi, preamble] There he met Nizam, the daughter of a transmitter of hadith, who inspired the poems of the Tarjuman al-Ashwaq, and he married Fatima, the daughter of a Meccan notable. [@eor-ibn-al-arabi, preamble] He says that during the circling of the Kaʿba in 1202 a mysterious Youth inspired insights in him and told him to write them down, and these became the Meccan Revelations. [@eor-ibn-al-arabi, preamble] Later he claimed to be the Seal of Muhammadan Sainthood, the saint who holds all the inner meanings of Muhammad's message. [@eor-ibn-al-arabi, preamble]

At Mecca he met disciples of [[abd-al-qadir-al-jilani]] and Majd al-Din Ishaq, an adviser to the Seljuk court of Anatolia, who became his friend and patron. [@eor-ibn-al-arabi, preamble] The two went together to [[konya]] to meet the Seljuk sultan, in a year the Encyclopedia of Religion does not give, between his arrival at Mecca and his move to Damascus. [@eor-ibn-al-arabi, preamble] He lived for a time at Malatya, Majd al-Din's home town, and travelled the Levant as an adviser to rulers from Konya to [[baghdad]], Aleppo and Damascus. [@eor-ibn-al-arabi, preamble] When Majd al-Din died, Ibn al-Arabi took charge of his son Sadr al-Din Qunawi, who became his heir and his most influential disciple. [@eor-ibn-al-arabi, preamble]

## Damascus

He spent the last seventeen years of his life at [[damascus]], where the judge Ibn al-Zaki was his patron. [@eor-ibn-al-arabi, preamble] The article on Sufism says he settled there in 1223 and stayed until he died in 1240, and that his mausoleum is still a place of pilgrimage. [@eor-sufism, preamble] He was given the name Muhyi al-Din, "the reviver of the religion", which [[al-ghazali]] had also borne. [@eor-ibn-al-arabi, preamble]

## Thought

The Encyclopedia of Religion says he is generally known as the prime exponent of the Unity of Being (wahdat al-wujud), though the term was barely used in his own circle and was attached to his teaching later. [@eor-ibn-al-arabi, preamble] It suggests that the centre of his teaching is better described as the perfectibility of man, and that his main aim was to turn the believer into a knower (ʿarif). [@eor-ibn-al-arabi, preamble] He held that God and the world depend on each other, since without a creation God cannot be known as Creator. [@eor-ibn-al-arabi, preamble]

The article on Sufism treats wahdat al-wujud as the central idea of his system and notes that scholars have debated whether he meant a monism in which only the One exists. [@eor-sufism, preamble] On that reading he took the Ashʿari teaching that God is the only true agent to the conclusion that God is also the only true existent. [@eor-sufism, preamble] Creation is then the One shown in the many created beings, a mirror in which God comes to know himself. [@eor-sufism, preamble]

The teaching places in every generation a Perfect Human Being (al-insan al-kamil), who links Absolute Being with the created world. [@eor-sufism, preamble] Ibn al-Arabi held that God appears in full, and the meaning of the universe becomes clear, only in man, who is made in the divine image. [@eor-ibn-al-arabi, preamble] For him and his followers the goal of the path was wisdom more than love, the insight that one has always been united with the source of all being. [@eor-sufism, preamble]

## The Interpreter of Desires

The [[tarjuman-al-ashwaq]], or Interpreter of Desires, is a collection of love poems in the manner of the Arabic qasida, which the Encyclopedia of Religion reads as a longing for the Divine Beloved. [@eor-ibn-al-arabi, preamble] In the preface to the later recensions Ibn al-Arabi says it holds "the love-poems which I composed at Mecca" in the months of Rajab, Shaʿban and Ramadan of 611 AH. [@ibn-arabi-tarjuman-nicholson, Introduction] Dozy had dated the poems to 598 AH, the year of the author's arrival at Mecca, but Nicholson rejects that date and concludes that the poems were finished in January 1215. [@ibn-arabi-tarjuman-nicholson, Introduction]

The mystical reading is the author's own claim. In his first preface he writes that in these poems he always signifies "Divine influences and spiritual revelations and sublime analogies". [@ibn-arabi-tarjuman-nicholson, Introduction] Some devout Muslims condemned the poems as "vain and amatorious", and a theologian at Aleppo doubted the author's claim, so Ibn al-Arabi wrote a commentary there that explains them line by line. [@ibn-arabi-tarjuman-nicholson, Introduction] Nicholson judges the claim sincere, while granting that some of the poems cannot be told apart from ordinary love songs. [@ibn-arabi-tarjuman-nicholson, Introduction] One poem has the lines "My heart has become capable of every form" and "I follow the religion of Love". [@ibn-arabi-tarjuman-nicholson, Poem_11]

## The Meccan Revelations

The article on Sufism says he was ordered in a vision during the pilgrimage to write the [[futuhat-al-makkiyya]], or Meccan Revelations. [@eor-sufism, preamble] It fills thirty-seven volumes. [@eor-ibn-al-arabi, preamble] He finished its first draft at Damascus in 1231 and a second recension of the whole in 1238. [@eor-ibn-al-arabi, preamble] Groups of friends and disciples, sometimes thirty or more, heard it read aloud in sections while the author checked the text, and the names of those present were written down in listening certificates. [@eor-ibn-al-arabi, preamble]

## The Bezels of Wisdom

The Encyclopedia of Religion calls the [[fusus-al-hikam]], or Bezels of Wisdom, "the quintessence of his teachings". [@eor-ibn-al-arabi, preamble] Ibn al-Arabi said the Prophet Muhammad handed it to him in a dream in 1229. [@eor-ibn-al-arabi, preamble] Each of its twenty-seven chapters treats one kind of wisdom through a prophet whose story the Quran tells. [@eor-ibn-al-arabi, preamble] At least a hundred commentaries on it were written, in several languages. [@eor-ibn-al-arabi, preamble]

## Followers and Critics

No dervish order was founded in his name, but his teachings shaped much of Sufi teaching after him. [@eor-ibn-al-arabi, preamble] The article on Sufism holds that from the thirteenth century on, Sufi theory took its form from the work of Ibn al-Arabi and his interpreters. [@eor-sufism, preamble] Sadr al-Din Qunawi taught at Konya at the same time as his friend [[rumi]] and handed Ibn al-Arabi's teaching on, and later followers included ʿAbd al-Razzaq al-Kashani and ʿAbd al-Rahman Jami. [@eor-ibn-al-arabi, preamble]

In India [[ahmad-sirhindi]], a Sufi of the [[naqshbandiyya]], wrote on the unity of being and the unity of appearance (wahdat al-shuhud). [@eor-sirhindi, preamble] The Encyclopedia of Religion places him in "the stream of mystical thought established by Ibn al-ʿArabī" while noting that the two differ on some points. [@eor-sirhindi, preamble]

The Ottoman Empire and Iran at times adopted his works publicly, but many Muslims have rejected them as heretical. [@eor-ibn-al-arabi, preamble] Some later scholars attacked his teaching as dangerous philosophizing, others defended his orthodoxy, and the dispute continues today. [@eor-ibn-al-arabi, preamble]
