# Qadiriyya

The Qadiriyya is a Sufi brotherhood, or tariqa, within [[sufism]]. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] The Encyclopedia of Religion says it is "commonly viewed as the first of the brotherhoods" to take the form of a structured organization, and that it was still active in the early twenty-first century. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] Its entry on the Hanbali school calls Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani the head of "the first Ṣūfī order in Islam that has come down to this day". [@eor-hanabilah, preamble]

## Origins in Baghdad

The order began in [[baghdad]]. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] It claims [[abd-al-qadir-al-jilani]] as its founder and traces its silsila, the chain of masters behind it, back through [[junayd]]. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] The Encyclopedia of Religion describes a tariqa in general as a confraternity formed around a charismatic master "or the memory" of one. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] Al-Jilani's tomb in Baghdad has stayed a place of pilgrimage for members of the order into the twenty-first century. [@eor-tariqah, preamble]

The madrasah al-Jilani directed in Baghdad has been kept up on the city's east side to the present. [@eor-hanabilah, preamble] One of the order's manuals describes a model initiation ceremony in which the candidate clasps the shaykh's right hand, recognising it as the hand of Abd al-Qadir. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] That rite is described on [The Tariqa as an Institution](islam/sufism/Tariqa.md).

## Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani

Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani came from Gilan, near the Caspian Sea in Iran. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] He was a legal scholar of the [[hanbali]] school. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] The Encyclopedia of Religion names him with Ibn Hubayrah and Ibn al-Jawzi as the Hanbalis who "dominate the twelfth century". [@eor-hanabilah, preamble]

His dates are given differently in the sources, and they are not reconciled here. The entry on the tariqa gives 1088 to 1166. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] The entry on Islam in South Asia gives 1077 to 1176 in its account of the orders' arrival, and a death in 1166 in a later section. [@eor-islam-south-asia, preamble] The Hanbali entry also has him die in 1166. [@eor-hanabilah, preamble] The entry on Sufism gives his death as 1116. [@eor-sufism, preamble]

He studied law under al-Mukharrimi, Ibn ʿAqil and others. [@eor-hanabilah, preamble] Al-Mukharrimi had built the first Hanbali madrasah in Baghdad, in the Bab al-Azaj quarter on the east side, at some point between 1102 and 1117. [@eor-hanabilah, preamble] A recently published silsila shows al-Mukharrimi passing the Sufi cloak of initiation, the khirqah, to al-Jilani. [@eor-hanabilah, preamble] Al-Jilani succeeded him at the madrasah as administrator, professor of law and Sufi master, and enlarged it into a madrasah and ribat for jurists and Sufis. [@eor-hanabilah, preamble] He also studied Sufism under Hammad al-Dabbas (d. 1131). [@eor-hanabilah, preamble]

His contemporary Ibn al-Jawzi attacked him, while Ibn Qudamah, ʿAbd al-Ghani al-Maqdisi and Ibn Taymiyah defended him. [@eor-hanabilah, preamble] Ibn Qudamah and Ibn Taymiyah are known to have belonged to his order. [@eor-hanabilah, preamble] The Encyclopedia of Religion calls him "perhaps the most famous saint in the Islamic world", and says stories of his miracles are told from Java to Morocco. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] In popular belief he renewed Islam, and members of the order tell of a dying man he revived who then revealed that he was the religion of Islam. [@eor-tariqah, preamble]

## Spread

From Baghdad the order spread as far as Southeast Asia, India, West Africa and the Maghreb, and into Sudan, Egypt and Yemen. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] It drew members from every level of society, "from ruler to peasant". [@eor-tariqah, preamble]

The sources differ on when it reached South Asia. The entry on the tariqa says the order was introduced to the subcontinent in the late fourteenth century. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] The entry on Islam in South Asia places the arrival of the Qadiriyya among the orders that appeared there from about the middle of the fifteenth century. [@eor-islam-south-asia, preamble]

Qadiri Sufis had settled at [[bidar]] around 1422, when it became the capital of the Bahmani sultanate. [@eor-islam-south-asia, preamble] Later Muhammad Ghawth (d. 1517), who claimed to be tenth in succession from al-Jilani, settled at [[uchch]]. [@eor-islam-south-asia, preamble] The [[bijapur]] sultanate became a major Qadiri centre, and in the seventeenth century the order accepted land grants there. [@eor-islam-south-asia, preamble] Al-Jilani has many shrines in southern India, though he is buried in Baghdad. [@eor-islam-south-asia, preamble]
