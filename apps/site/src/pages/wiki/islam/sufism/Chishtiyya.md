# Chishtiyya

The Chishtiyya is a Sufi order of South Asia and a branch of [[sufism]]. With the [[suhrawardiyya]] it was one of the earliest orders at work in India, and the Encyclopedia of Religion calls it "the first to originate in the subcontinent". [@eor-tariqah, preamble] The same encyclopedia's article on Sufism names "the music-loving Chishtīyah" among the great orders that joined mainstream Sufism from the thirteenth century on. [@eor-sufism, preamble] On how orders are organized, see [The Tariqa as an Institution](islam/sufism/Tariqa.md).

## Muin al-Din Chishti

[[muin-al-din-chishti]] founded the order and died in 1236. He came from Sistan and had spent some time as a disciple of Abu Najib al-Suhrawardi. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] The Encyclopedia of Religion's article on the tariqa says he reached [[delhi]] in 1193 and then went on to [[ajmer]], a major city of Rajputana, which had just been conquered, and founded a khanaqah there. [@eor-tariqah, preamble]

The encyclopedia's article on Islam in South Asia dates his arrival differently. It says the order came from Afghanistan with Khwajah Muin ad-Din, who "settled in Ajmer (Rajasthan) in the 1290s". [@eor-islam-south-asia, preamble] That is decades after the death in 1236 that the tariqa article gives, and the two articles do not agree. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] The South Asia article adds that his successor, Qutb ad-Din Bakhtiyar Kaki, who died in 1235, carried Chishti influence to Delhi. [@eor-islam-south-asia, preamble]

Muin al-Din is buried at Ajmer, in what the South Asia article calls the "mother" dargah of the order. [@eor-islam-south-asia, preamble] Tradition holds that he won many followers once he began to use music in his khanqah. [@eor-islam-south-asia, preamble]

## Nizam al-Din Awliya

[[nizam-al-din-awliya]] (1238 to 1325) was a major Sufi saint of the Chishti order. [@eor-nizam-al-din-awliya, preamble] His grandparents on both sides had fled the Mongols from Bukhara and settled in Badaon, east of Delhi, where he was born. [@eor-nizam-al-din-awliya, preamble] At sixteen he saw that he could advance no further as a scholar in Badaon and moved with his family to Delhi. [@eor-nizam-al-din-awliya, preamble] Stories of Baba Farid al-Din Ganj-i Shakar, then head of the Chishti order, drew him to Ajodhan, now Pak Pattan in the Punjab, where he became Baba Farid's disciple. [@eor-nizam-al-din-awliya, preamble]

According to traditional accounts of his life, Baba Farid named him successor when he was twenty-three and told him to found a khanqah in Delhi. [@eor-nizam-al-din-awliya, preamble] He settled at Ghiyathpur, a village on the Jumna a short way outside the capital, and his khanqah grew from a straw hut into the largest in India. [@eor-nizam-al-din-awliya, preamble] The tariqa article says he spent fifty years spreading the order across India and sent out hundreds of disciples from his Delhi centre. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] The entry on Nizam al-Din says the order became a mass movement across India under his leadership. [@eor-nizam-al-din-awliya, preamble] The South Asia article pairs him with Nasir ad-Din Mahmud, called Chiragh-i Dihli, as "the two great shaykh s of fourteenth-century Delhi", and says that in their lifetimes branches of the order were founded in Bengal, Daulatabad and Gulbarga. [@eor-islam-south-asia, preamble]

His khanqah kept the order's rule against ties to political power. Senior disciples could not take government posts, and the khanqah refused gifts with conditions and grants of land or regular income. [@eor-nizam-al-din-awliya, preamble] Court scholars intrigued against him because he allowed music as an aid to religious rapture, which they opposed, but the order kept growing. [@eor-nizam-al-din-awliya, preamble]

He died on April 3, 1325, and his grave is in the khanqah garden. [@eor-nizam-al-din-awliya, preamble] The sultan Muhammad Tughluq was a pallbearer at his funeral and built the mausoleum over the grave. [@eor-nizam-al-din-awliya, preamble]

## Practice

The Chishti shaykhs in India lived and taught in their own houses, each called a "community home" (jamāʿat-khānah), so that their adepts would not be drawn into running a large centre and its endowments. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] The tariqa article puts the order's wide following down to its plain and fervent teaching, its hospitality and charity, and its welcome to every guest. [@eor-tariqah, preamble]

The order actively encouraged samaʿ, the hearing of music, and other South Asian orders followed it. Qawwali, a genre of Sufi music, grew out of this practice. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] The [[naqshbandiyya]], by contrast, recited the dhikr silently and banned music. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] Chishti shaykhs also promoted devotional poetry on mystical themes in the local languages. [@eor-islam-south-asia, preamble] ʿAbd al-Rahman, a Chishti of Awrangzib's reign, is regarded as the greatest mystical poet in Pashto. [@eor-tariqah, preamble]

## Royal Patronage

The early Chishtis kept away from rulers, since they held that close contact with political power harmed a person's moral and spiritual life. [@eor-islam-south-asia, preamble] The Suhrawardiyya, by contrast, had great influence in the court circles of thirteenth-century Delhi. [@eor-sufism, preamble] By the early fourteenth century the Chishtiyya too was rising on the strength of the royal patronage it drew. [@eor-islam-south-asia, preamble]

The Mughal emperors were its most generous patrons. They endowed the Ajmer dargah, paid for building there and appointed its administrators. [@eor-islam-south-asia, preamble] Akbar, who died in 1605, made fourteen pilgrimages to the shrine. Two of them, in 1568 and 1574, came straight after his conquests of Chittor and Bengal, which he credited to the blessings of Muin al-Din. [@eor-islam-south-asia, preamble] Jahangir adorned Ajmer with buildings of white marble, and his granddaughter Jahanara Begum, who died in 1681, wrote about Muin al-Din's life and asked to be buried in his shrine compound. [@eor-tariqah, preamble]

Akbar's heir Salim, later Jahangir, was born in the home of a Chishti shaykh, and Akbar paid for a dargah for the order at [[fatehpur-sikri]] in thanks. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] The South Asia article says Shaykh Salim Chishti, a descendant of Muin al-Din, foretold the birth, and that Akbar built his new capital, Fatehpur Sikri, near Salim Chishti's khanqah. [@eor-islam-south-asia, preamble] In the seventeenth century the Naqshbandiyya competed with the Chishtiyya for the Mughals' attention. [@eor-islam-south-asia, preamble]
