# Sufism

Sufism is the mystical tradition of [[islam]]. [@eor-sufism, preamble] The name most likely comes from the Arabic word for wool, ṣūf, after the coarse woollen clothes the early ascetics are said to have worn as a sign that they had turned from the world. [@eor-sufism, preamble] Its first centres in Central Asia were [[balkh]] and [[nishapur]], in the eighth and ninth centuries, and later [[merv]], [[bukhara]] and other cities of Transoxiana became Sufi strongholds. [@eor-islam-central-asia, preamble]

This page follows the tradition in time order, from the ascetics of the eighth century through the Baghdad masters and the manual writers to the orders. Each person or order with a page of its own is linked from here.

## The Ascetics

The Encyclopedia of Religion lists a reaction against the wealth and worldliness of the Umayyad caliphate among the early catalysts of the ascetic movement. [@eor-sufism, preamble] In the eighth and ninth centuries its main centres were Iraq, above all [[basra]], [[kufa|Kufa]] and [[baghdad]], the province of Khorasan, especially Balkh, and Egypt. [@eor-sufism, preamble] As early as 850 Baghdad had samāʿ houses where Sufis could listen to music. [@eor-tariqah, preamble]

### Hasan al-Basri

[[hasan-al-basri]] (642 to 728) was an ascetic of the generation after the Prophet, born at [[medina]] and raised in nearby Wadi al-Qura. [@eor-hasan-al-basri, preamble] During the First Civil War he moved to Basra and settled there for good. [@eor-hasan-al-basri, preamble] He preached against worldliness and the hypocrisy it bred, and his sermons and letters warn that the life of this world passes and the hereafter lasts. [@eor-hasan-al-basri, preamble]

The Encyclopedia of Religion's article on him reports that "both the Ṣūfīs and the Muʿtazilah regarded Ḥasan as one of their forerunners". [@eor-hasan-al-basri, preamble] Al-Hujwiri, writing in the eleventh century, says he "is held in high regard and esteem by the Ṣúfís". [@hujwiri-kashf-al-mahjub-nicholson, §10.3] The Encyclopedia's article on Sufism counts him among the early figures who shaped the tradition, and also names him as a transmitter of hadith and a defender of human free will. [@eor-sufism, preamble]

### Rabia

[[rabia]] al-Adawiyya died in 801. [@eor-rabiah, preamble] For a time she was a house servant in Basra, and her master freed her because of her piety. [@eor-rabiah, preamble] The Encyclopedia of Religion says she is regarded as the person who introduced the idea of pure love of God into the ascetic life of the second Islamic century. [@eor-rabiah, preamble] Her concern was ascetic practice as a means to a loving bond with God, and she held that making the practices themselves the goal distorted the Sufi path. [@eor-sufism, preamble]

Stories connect her with Hasan al-Basri, and some claim he proposed marriage to her. The article on her calls these stories "pure invention", since Hasan died in 728 when she was about ten. [@eor-rabiah, preamble] The article on Sufism puts her at "at best in her early teens" that year. [@eor-sufism, preamble]

### Other Early Figures

Ibrahim ibn Adham (d. 770?) was said to be a prince of Balkh who gave up his throne for the ascetic path, though the sources for his life are hard to separate from pious legend. [@eor-sufism, preamble] Dhu al-Nun al-Misri (d. 859) lived in Cairo and travelled widely, and later writers kept many of his sayings, prayers and poems. [@eor-sufism, preamble]

## The Baghdad Masters

Later Sufis set the "intoxication" of Abu Yazid al-Bistami against the "sobriety" of [[junayd]] of Baghdad (d. 910), and the Encyclopedia of Religion says that division lasted through the whole history of Islamic mysticism. [@eor-sufism, preamble] [[al-hallaj]], born in 857, trained under Junayd among other masters before he broke away and became a wandering preacher. [@eor-sufism, preamble] He was executed in 922 after about eight years in prison. [@eor-sufism, preamble]

## The Manual Tradition

From the late ninth century, manuals appeared that were written for a wider Muslim public and served as training guides for those who wanted to cultivate mystical experience. [@eor-sufism, preamble] The Encyclopedia of Religion says they stressed that Sufism was open to all and agreed with orthodox Islam, and that they never replaced the bond between master and disciple. [@eor-sufism, preamble]

### Al-Qushayri

The classics of the genre include [[risala-qushayriyya]], the Qushayrian Letter of [[al-qushayri]], who died in 1074. [@eor-sufism, preamble] He and al-Kalabadhi set out to show that Sufism agreed with Ashʿarism, the orthodox theology of their day. [@eor-sufism, preamble]

### Al-Hujwiri

[[al-hujwiri]] wrote [[kashf-al-mahjub]], an early Persian textbook of Sufism. [@eor-hujwiri, preamble] He came from Ghaznin and was born around the start of the eleventh century. [@eor-hujwiri, preamble] Nicholson, who translated the Kashf, notes that he "was a Sunní and a Ḥanafite", a [[sunni]] of the [[hanafi]] school. [@hujwiri-kashf-al-mahjub-nicholson, preamble] He studied Sufism with Abu al-Fadl al-Khuttali and travelled widely, and among the places he names is [[merv|Merv]]. [@hujwiri-kashf-al-mahjub-nicholson, preamble] He says he was parted from his books in Ghaznin and held among strangers in [[lahore]]. [@eor-hujwiri, preamble]

Nicholson writes that the Kashf "contains a Persian rendering of some passages" of al-Qushayri's Risala, and thinks the two men may have met. [@hujwiri-kashf-al-mahjub-nicholson, preamble] He judges the Kashf a full account of Sufi doctrine and practice, where the Risala is mostly a collection of "sayings, anecdotes, and definitions". [@hujwiri-kashf-al-mahjub-nicholson, preamble]

The sources disagree on when al-Hujwiri died. The Encyclopedia of Religion heads its article "d. ah 469?/1076? ce" and adds that the year AH 465 on his tomb is probably close. [@eor-hujwiri, preamble] Nicholson reasons that he probably outlived al-Qushayri, who died in 1072 by his reckoning, and conjectures a death between AH 465 and 469. [@hujwiri-kashf-al-mahjub-nicholson, preamble] The Encyclopedia's article on Islam in South Asia says he "settled and died in Lahore in 1071", and elsewhere in the same article puts his death in Lahore between 1072 and 1077. [@eor-islam-south-asia, preamble]

They also disagree on where he wrote the Kashf. The Encyclopedia of Religion reads his past tense to mean he had left Lahore when he wrote it and was then probably in Ghaznin, and for that reason doubts that his tomb in Lahore is genuine. [@eor-hujwiri, preamble] Nicholson places the work in the author's later years and, "partly at any rate, to the period of his residence in Lahore". [@hujwiri-kashf-al-mahjub-nicholson, preamble]

### Al-Ghazali

The Encyclopedia of Religion's article on Sufism sees the manual writers' effort to win Sufism a place in Islam culminating in [[al-ghazali]]. [@eor-sufism, preamble] After a crisis in 1095 he left his teaching post at Baghdad and turned to Sufism for an answer to his doubts. [@eor-sufism, preamble] The article on him says that his life and writing led mainstream Sunni scholars to look more kindly on the Sufis, and opened moderate Sufi practice to ordinary Muslims. [@eor-ghazali, preamble]

## The Orders

From the thirteenth century many Sufi groups no longer broke up when their master died. They became lasting bodies centred on a founder and his teaching, with authority handed from master to disciple. [@eor-sufism, preamble] These groups were called ṭarīqahs, "ways", and each founder had his silsilah, a chain of masters going back through the generations. [@eor-sufism, preamble] Several orders date their growth into formal institutions to the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] How they worked, with their lodges, rites and endowments, is the subject of [The Tariqa as an Institution](islam/sufism/Tariqa.md).

The four orders that have pages here are listed by the date of their first centre.

- **[[qadiriyya]]** - began in Baghdad and claims [[abd-al-qadir-al-jilani]] as its founder. [@eor-tariqah, preamble]
- **[[chishtiyya]]** - the Encyclopedia of Religion's article on the orders says its founder Muʿin al-Din Chishti reached [[delhi]] in 1193 and moved on to Ajmer. [@eor-tariqah, preamble] The article on Islam in South Asia instead has him settle at Ajmer in the 1290s. [@eor-islam-south-asia, preamble]
- **[[mevlevi]]** - Rumi's disciples at [[konya]] were organised into an order by his son Sultan Walad, who died in 1312. [@eor-tariqah, preamble]
- **[[naqshbandiyya]]** - founded at Bukhara by Baha al-Din Naqshband (1318 to 1388). [@eor-tariqah, preamble]

The theory of Sufism from the thirteenth century on was shaped by [[ibn-arabi]] and his interpreters. [@eor-sufism, preamble] The poet [[rumi]], whose disciples became the Mevlevi, was born at Balkh in 1207. [@eor-sufism, preamble]
