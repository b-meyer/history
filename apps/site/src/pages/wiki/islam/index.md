# Islam

Muslims trace their tradition to [[muhammad]], whom they revere as the prophet to whom the Qurʾān was revealed. [@eor-muhammad, preamble] The Encyclopedia of Religion's entry on the sunnah, the example of the Prophet, notes that in every "founded" tradition closeness to the founder gives legitimacy and authority, and that in Islam these questions gather around the word sunnah. [@eor-sunnah, preamble] Muhammad led the Muslim community at [[medina]] from the migration (hijrah) of 622 until his death there in 632. [@eor-muhammad, preamble]

## The Branches

The Encyclopedia of Religion says that when the Umayyad dynasty took power in 661, the [[shia]] and the [[khawarij]] rejected it and broke from the majority of Muslims, who came to be called [[sunni|Sunnis]]. [@eor-islamic-law-shariah, preamble] The Sunnis accepted the leadership of the first four caliphs, the "Rightly Guided" ones, as legitimate. [@eor-madhhab, preamble]

The Shia take their name from the "party" of [[ali]], the cousin of Muhammad and husband of his daughter Fatimah. [@eor-shiism-overview, preamble] They held that Ali had a better claim to lead the community than any of the three caliphs before him. [@eor-madhhab, preamble] The Encyclopedia of Religion has the Shia emerge during the First Civil War as supporters of Ali's caliphate, which lasted from 656 to 661, and its entry on the law schools puts the split at about 660. [@eor-shiism-overview, preamble] [@eor-madhhab, preamble] After Ali was murdered in 661, the Shia kept up their opposition to the Umayyads from [[kufa]], his former capital in Iraq. [@eor-shiism-overview, preamble] The killing of his son Husayn at [[karbala]] in 680 gave rise to a radical wing of the movement. [@eor-shiism-overview, preamble]

The [[ismaili|Ismailis]] are a major branch of the Shia who trace the line of imams through Ismail, son of the imam Jafar al-Sadiq. [@eor-shiism-ismailiyah, preamble] Ismail died before his father, who died in about 765, and the followers who held to Ismail's designation became the founders of the Ismaili line. [@eor-shiism-ismailiyah, preamble] [@eor-shiism-overview, preamble] The Ismailis set up the Fatimid caliphate at [[cairo]], which lasted until Saladin overthrew it in 1171. [@eor-shiism-ithna-ashariyah, preamble] [@eor-shiism-ismailiyah, preamble] In 1090 the Ismaili leader Hasan-i Sabbah seized the mountain fortress of [[alamut]], northwest of Qazvin, and made it his headquarters. [@eor-shiism-ismailiyah, preamble] The Mongols conquered Alamut in 1256. [@eor-shiism-overview, preamble]

The Khawarij are the "third party" in Islam, who condemn both the Sunnis and the Shia. [@eor-kharijis, preamble] The Encyclopedia of Religion traces them to the arbitration that Ali accepted with his rival Muawiyah after the Battle of Siffin in 657. [@eor-kharijis, preamble] A group of Ali's followers held that agreeing to it was a great sin, left his camp and gathered at Harura near Kufa. [@eor-kharijis, preamble] Their name means "those who went out". [@eor-kharijis, preamble]

## The Sunni Law Schools

Sunni Muslims came to accept four schools of law as equally orthodox. [@eor-madhhab, preamble] The [[hanafi|Hanafi]] school grew up in the eighth century among the jurists of Kufa and is named after the Kufan jurist Abu Hanifa. [@eor-islamic-law-shariah, preamble] [@eor-madhhab, preamble] The [[maliki|Maliki]] school, the oldest of the four, began at Medina and is named after Malik ibn Anas, who spent almost his whole life there. [@eor-islamic-law-shariah, preamble] [@eor-madhhab, preamble] The [[shafii|Shafii]] school takes its name from Muhammad ibn Idris al-Shafii, a pupil of Malik who set out the classical theory of the roots of law. [@eor-madhhab, preamble] The [[hanbali|Hanbali]] school formed in the ninth century around Ahmad ibn Hanbal, a traditionist of [[baghdad]], and his students spread it beyond that city. [@eor-madhhab, preamble] [@eor-islamic-law-shariah, preamble] [@eor-hanabilah, preamble]
