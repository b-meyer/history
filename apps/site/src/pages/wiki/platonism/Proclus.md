# Proclus

Proclus was born in [[constantinople]] in 412 into a rich Lycian family [@sep-proclus, §1.1]. The Stanford article calls him Proclus of [[athens]] and gives his years as 412 to 485 [@sep-proclus, preamble]. Little of his own work records his life, so the account rests on the eulogy by his pupil Marinus of Neapolis, a biography written to praise its subject [@sep-proclus, §1]. Where this page gives Marinus's portents and dreams, they are his reports.

## Early Life

Marinus says the goddess Athena of Constantinople welcomed Proclus at his birth [@marinus-life-of-proclus-guthrie, Marinus 6]. Soon afterward his parents went home to [[xanthus]] in Lycia, where he began his schooling [@sep-proclus, §1.1]. He then went to [[alexandria]] to study rhetoric, intending to become a lawyer like his father [@sep-proclus, §1.1].

On a journey to Constantinople he found that philosophy was his vocation [@sep-proclus, §1.1]. Marinus says the goddess advised him on his arrival there to turn to philosophy and to attend the Athenian schools [@marinus-life-of-proclus-guthrie, Marinus 9]. Back in Alexandria he studied Aristotle and mathematics [@sep-proclus, §1.1]. Marinus names his teachers as the younger [[olympiodorus]] for Aristotle and [[heron]] for mathematics [@marinus-life-of-proclus-guthrie, Marinus 9].

## Athens

Proclus came to Athens in 430 or 431, aged eighteen, drawn by the fame of the Platonic school there [@sep-proclus, §1.1]. Marinus says the gods sent him to preserve the school of Plato, and he reports omens at the arrival, among them a gatekeeper who told Proclus he would have shut the gate had he not come [@marinus-life-of-proclus-guthrie, Marinus 10].

He studied for two years under [[plutarch-of-athens]], reading Plato's Phaedo and Aristotle's De anima [@sep-proclus, §1.1]. After Plutarch died in 432, [[syrianus]] became head of the school, and Proclus read first Aristotle and then the Platonic dialogues with him [@sep-proclus, §1.1]. Marinus says Syrianus took him through Aristotle's works in under two years and then led him to what Marinus calls the Greater Mysteries of Plato [@marinus-life-of-proclus-guthrie, Marinus 13]. Under Syrianus he also met the Orphic theology and the Chaldaean Oracles [@sep-proclus, §1.1].

Proclus succeeded Syrianus after his death in 437 and held the headship for almost fifty years, until 485 [@sep-proclus, §1.1]. The Stanford article calls the school the Platonic "Academy" and gives him the title of successor of Plato [@sep-proclus, preamble]. The [Academy](platonism/Academy.md) page covers the school itself and the dispute over its later history.

His days began with a prayer to the sun, repeated at noon and at sunset, and went on to lectures, reading seminars and discussions with students [@sep-proclus, §1.1]. Marinus portrays him as a practitioner of theurgy [@sep-proclus, §1.1]. Pagan rites could be kept up only privately on the school's grounds, and Proclus had to be careful to avoid anti-pagan reactions [@sep-proclus, §1.1]. Marinus says he left Athens after a time of suspicion and trouble, spent no more than a year in [[lydia]], and came back to Athens [@marinus-life-of-proclus-guthrie, Marinus 15].

## Death

Marinus gives the date of Proclus's death as the seventeenth of April in the archonship of the younger Nicagoras at Athens, which the translator equates with 485 [@marinus-life-of-proclus-guthrie, Marinus 36]. He was buried near [[lycabettus]], where his teacher Syrianus lay [@marinus-life-of-proclus-guthrie, Marinus 36]. Marinus also reports a solar eclipse a year before the death, which he took as a sign of the eclipse of philosophy [@marinus-life-of-proclus-guthrie, Marinus 37].

The Stanford article on Neoplatonism dates the disbanding of the Athenian Neoplatonists to the aftermath of Justinian's legislation of 529 [@sep-neoplatonism, §7]. That is the [[justinian-edict]] [@sep-neoplatonism, §7], which came after Proclus's death in 485 [@marinus-life-of-proclus-guthrie, Marinus 36].

## Writings

Marinus says that by the age of twenty-eight Proclus had written many treatises, among them a commentary on the Timaeus [@marinus-life-of-proclus-guthrie, Marinus 13]. The Stanford article cites the same passage for the age of 27 [@sep-proclus, §1.2]. Marinus adds that Proclus preferred this commentary, and that he said that of all ancient books he would leave in circulation only the Oracles and the Timaeus [@marinus-life-of-proclus-guthrie, Marinus 38].

Roughly two thirds of his output is lost [@sep-proclus, §1.2]. The surviving works include five commentaries on Plato, a commentary on Euclid, the Elements of Physics and the Elements of Theology, an astronomical work, the Tria opuscula, three monographs whose subjects are providence, fate, free will and the origin of evil, the Platonic Theology and a set of hymns [@sep-proclus, §1.2]. The Platonic Theology is generally taken to be his last work [@sep-proclus, §1.2].

The Stanford article calls the [[elements-of-theology]] probably the best starting point for the study of his philosophy, a systematic introduction to the Neoplatonic metaphysical system [@sep-proclus, preamble]. It opens with the proposition that all multitude participates in some respect of the one [@proclus-elements-of-theology-taylor, ET 1]. In Thomas Taylor's translation, proposition 129 sets out a chain of dependence. A divine body is divine through a deified soul, soul through a divine intellect, and intellect through participation of a divine unity [@proclus-elements-of-theology-taylor, ET 129].

## Place in the Tradition

The Stanford article describes Proclus as continuing a movement begun by [[iamblichus]] and as following the teaching of Syrianus [@sep-proclus, preamble]. In the same account, Proclus was eager to show that the religious revelations of Homer and Hesiod, the Orphic poems and the Chaldaean Oracles agree with one another, and to bring them into the philosophical tradition of Pythagoras and Plato [@sep-proclus, preamble]. Marinus says Proclus read the works of Porphyry and Iamblichus on the Oracles [@marinus-life-of-proclus-guthrie, Marinus 26].

The Stanford article on Neoplatonism lists him among the important figures of late antique [[neoplatonists|Neoplatonism]] [@sep-neoplatonism, §1]. His student [[ammonius-hermeiou|Ammonius]] became head of the school at Alexandria [@sep-proclus, preamble].
