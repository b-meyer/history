# The Theology of Aristotle

The Theology of Aristotle is the longest and best known text that keeps an Arabic version of the [[enneads]] of [[plotinus]]. [@sep-theology-aristotle, §1] Plotinus was put into Arabic in the ninth century, and long sections of that translation went under the title Theology of Aristotle. [@sep-theology-aristotle, preamble] The work is not by [[aristotle]]. The Stanford Encyclopedia counts it among the Neoplatonic works "falsely attributed to Aristotle" in al-Kindi's time, [@sep-arabic-islamic-greek, §3] and its entry on Neoplatonism calls it a pseudo-epigraphical treatise. [@sep-neoplatonism, §8]

## The Text

The Theology is divided into ten sections, each called a mimar, a Syriac word for a chapter, and opens with a preface and a set of "headings" that seem to list points from Enneads IV.4. [@sep-theology-aristotle, §1] Every passage in it comes from Enneads IV, V and VI, and no direct evidence shows an Arabic version of any of the first three Enneads. [@sep-theology-aristotle, §1] The passages do not keep the order of the Enneads but move from one part of them to another. [@sep-theology-aristotle, §1]

Two other surviving texts come from the same Arabic version of Plotinus. One is the Letter on Divine Science, which was falsely ascribed to al-Farabi, and the other is a group of sayings ascribed to a "Greek sage". [@sep-theology-aristotle, §1] The Arabic is often a free paraphrase, and it carries interpolated passages, some as long as a paragraph, with no direct basis in the Greek. [@sep-theology-aristotle, §1] For that reason the Stanford Encyclopedia treats the Theology as an interpretation of Plotinus's thought and not only a translation. [@sep-theology-aristotle, preamble]

The Theology follows [[porphyry]]'s edition of the Enneads, since it keeps the breaks Porphyry made when he split longer treatises to bring the count to 54. [@sep-theology-aristotle, §2] The Greek copy behind the translation was older than the oldest Greek manuscripts of the Enneads now extant and belonged to an independent branch of the tradition. The third chapter of the Theology has a section of Enneads IV.7.8 that every Greek manuscript lacks and that Eusebius transmits indirectly. [@sep-theology-aristotle, §2]

## The Preface

The preface opens by calling the work "the book of Aristotle the philosopher", and the title comes from it. [@sep-theology-aristotle, §2] It goes on to name "the exposition of Porphyry of Syria", a translation into Arabic by Ibn Na'ima al-Himsi, and a correction by [[al-kindi]] made for Ahmad ibn al-Mu'tasim. [@sep-theology-aristotle, §2] Ahmad was the son of the Abbasid caliph who reigned from 833 to 842, which the Stanford Encyclopedia takes as "a fairly reliable date" for the making of the Theology, or at least for al-Kindi's correction. [@sep-theology-aristotle, §2]

Al-Himsi was a Syrian Christian translator of al-Kindi's circle who also put the Sophistical Refutations and parts of the Physics of Aristotle into Arabic. [@sep-theology-aristotle, §2] Whether he worked from the Greek or through a Syriac version is disputed. Zimmermann sees no need for a Syriac intermediary, Bucur and Bucur have questioned that view, and Brock has argued against the intermediary. [@sep-theology-aristotle, §2]

The mention of Porphyry may refer only to the fact that the Arabic version was based on his edition. Some scholars have read the Arabic Plotinus as a translation of a commentary by Porphyry, but Zimmermann rejected this hypothesis and later scholarship has followed him. [@sep-theology-aristotle, §2] D'Ancona and Chase have argued that the "headings" may still go back to Porphyry's lost index of the main points of the Enneads. [@sep-theology-aristotle, §2]

Who in the circle reworked the text is also disputed. Zimmermann and Adamson give the main part to al-Himsi and little to al-Kindi, while D'Ancona has suggested that al-Kindi had a larger role. [@sep-theology-aristotle, §2]

## The Name of Aristotle

Why the work came to bear Aristotle's name is an open question. Some have proposed that the misattribution was deliberate, to win readers or authority for the text, while Zimmermann held that it was a later and ill-informed addition based on a misreading of the rest of the preface. [@sep-theology-aristotle, §2] The preface presents the Theology as saying more about God, His relation to the world and the immaterial soul than Aristotle himself does. On Zimmermann's reading, which the Stanford Encyclopedia finds plausible, the Kindi circle thought Plotinus agreed with Aristotle and even completed him, without believing that Aristotle wrote the book. [@sep-theology-aristotle, §2]

The author of the opening speaks as if he were Aristotle and says he has turned to the First Cause, the Intellect and the Soul after treating matter, form and the causes in his Metaphysics. [@sep-arabic-islamic-greek, §3] The Stanford Encyclopedia holds that this claimed authorship gave Plotinus's teaching on the One, the intelligible world and the soul a large place in later Arabic philosophy, above all in the eastern Islamic world. [@sep-arabic-islamic-greek, §3]

## Ideas of the Arabic Plotinus

In the Arabic version the One is a creating God, often called "originator" and "creator", and the text still uses the language of emanation without any sense of conflict. [@sep-theology-aristotle, §4.2] It also lets the First Cause think or be an intellect, an idea Plotinus rejected or mentioned only with great care. [@sep-theology-aristotle, §4.2] A passage that calls God "pure actuality" seems to show the influence of book 12 of Aristotle's Metaphysics, which the Kindi circle had translated. The Stanford Encyclopedia adds that such passages may only bring out the Aristotelian side of Plotinus himself. [@sep-theology-aristotle, §4.2]

## Al-Kindi's Circle and Proclus

The Kindi circle set out to promote Greek philosophy and science and to make them Islamic. [@sep-theology-aristotle, §3] Besides Plotinus it translated works of Aristotle and of [[proclus]]. [@sep-theology-aristotle, §3] An Arabic version of Proclus's [[elements-of-theology]] seems to have existed, and it survives only in later partial reworkings, the best known being the Book on the Pure Good, called the Book of Causes (Liber de causis) in Latin. [@sep-theology-aristotle, §3] The Book of Causes had little influence in Arabic but was a major source of Neoplatonic ideas in the Latin west, and Thomas Aquinas identified it as based on Proclus. [@sep-theology-aristotle, §3]

D'Ancona and others have argued that the Arabic Plotinus directly shaped the Arabic Proclus, which reduces the system of the Elements to the hierarchy of the Arabic Plotinus, with God, Intellect, Soul and the material world. [@sep-theology-aristotle, §3] The Stanford Encyclopedia also reports a suggestion by D'Ancona that the Elements helped inspire the program of study set out in the Theology's preface. [@sep-theology-aristotle, §3] Zimmermann proposed that the Arabic Plotinus and Proclus were parts of one large "metaphysics file" that the circle assembled. [@sep-theology-aristotle, §3]

## The Translation Movement

Before the rise of Islam, the Christian elites of Syria had taken up Greek philosophy, much of it Aristotelian. [@sep-arabic-islamic-greek, §1] Aristotle's logical writings and Porphyry's Isagoge were translated into Syriac. [@sep-arabic-islamic-greek, §1] Under the Umayyads the Christian communities of Syria went on studying Greek philosophy and science, and their learning lay behind the Syrian Christians who took part in the translation movement of the early Abbasid caliphate. [@sep-arabic-islamic-greek, §1]

Some Greek works of loosely philosophical content were already translated into Arabic at Damascus under the Umayyads (661 to 750), but the translations flourished under the Abbasids, above all in their first two centuries. [@sep-arabic-islamic-greek, §2] By the reign of al-Mansur (754 to 775), who founded [[baghdad]] in 762, an outline of Aristotle's logic in its late antique form was available in Arabic. [@sep-arabic-islamic-greek, §2]

Many translations made under al-Ma'mun (813 to 833) and his successors survive. [@sep-arabic-islamic-greek, §3] The court library, the House of Wisdom, was not an institutional center for the translations, but the first group of translators had close ties to the court, and al-Kindi led that group. [@sep-arabic-islamic-greek, §3] Endress showed in 1973 that the circle's translations share lexical and syntactic features. [@sep-arabic-islamic-greek, §3] Among the authors they put into Arabic were Plato, Aristotle, Plotinus and Proclus, along with Nicomachus of Gerasa, Alexander of Aphrodisias, John Philoponus and Olympiodorus. [@sep-arabic-islamic-greek, §3] Around 840 parts of Enneads IV to VI, which Porphyry's edition gives to the soul, the intelligible world and the One, were translated into Arabic. [@sep-arabic-islamic-greek, §3] Plotinus circulated under the name of Aristotle, and Proclus under the names of Aristotle and Alexander of Aphrodisias. [@sep-arabic-islamic-greek, §3]

Around the middle of the tenth century a further set of translations came from the Nestorian Christian Abu Bishr Matta ibn Yunus and his pupils. [@sep-arabic-islamic-greek, §5] In Baghdad his pupils included [[al-farabi]] and Yahya ibn 'Adi, and he knew no Greek, so he worked from Syriac versions already in existence. [@sep-arabic-islamic-greek, §5]

Most scholars hold that the translation movement gave rise to falsafa, the Arabic philosophical tradition, while others trace falsafa to the Qur'anic idea of wisdom. [@sep-arabic-islamic-greek, preamble]

## Readers of the Theology

The Theology became the main source of Neoplatonic ideas in Arabic. [@sep-theology-aristotle, §5] The Stanford Encyclopedia's entry on Neoplatonism names al-Kindi, al-Farabi and [[ibn-sina]] as the thinkers through whom it helped bring ancient philosophy and science into Islam. [@sep-neoplatonism, §8]

The Stanford Encyclopedia's entry on al-Kindi says that influences from the Theology can be detected in his On First Philosophy. [@sep-al-kindi, §2.1] The Harmony of the Two Sages, a work ascribed to al-Farabi, cites the Theology to show that Plato and Aristotle agree on the timeless creation of the universe and on the Forms. [@sep-theology-aristotle, §5] Under that title al-Farabi includes propositions from the Arabic Proclus, so he had a larger work in mind than the Theology that survives. [@sep-theology-aristotle, §5]

Ibn Sina doubted that the work was authentic but still wrote notes commenting on it. [@sep-theology-aristotle, §5] His lost Fair Judgment, a detailed commentary on the "difficult passages" of the entire Aristotelian corpus, took in the Theology, and two incomplete recensions of its commentary on the Theology survive. [@sep-ibn-sina, §1.2]

Its influence may have been highest in the tenth century, when Abu Ya'qub al-Sijistani, al-Kirmani and other Isma'ili writers fitted Plotinus's hierarchy to their own metaphysics and Miskawayh's Shorter Healing is full of terminology and doctrine from the Arabic Plotinus. [@sep-theology-aristotle, §5] A Longer Version in fourteen chapters reworked the text further, most likely in Fatimid Egypt, and Pier Nicola Castellani's Latin translation of 1519 was made from it. [@sep-theology-aristotle, §5] Many manuscripts of the Theology were copied in Iran under the Safavids and Qajars, and later Safavid thinkers, Mulla Sadra among them, wrote commentaries on it. [@sep-theology-aristotle, §5]
