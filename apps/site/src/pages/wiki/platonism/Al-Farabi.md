# al-Farabi

Little that is really reliable is known about the life of Abu Nasr al-Farabi [@sep-al-farabi, preamble]. He was probably born in 870, at a place called Farab or Farayb [@sep-al-farabi, preamble]. As a young man he moved to Iraq and to [[baghdad]] [@sep-al-farabi, preamble]. There, in the first decades of the tenth century, he was a pupil of Abu Bishr Matta ibn Yunus, a Nestorian Christian who translated Aristotle into Arabic from the Syriac, alongside the Jacobite Christian Yahya ibn Adi [@sep-arabic-islamic-greek, §5]. In 943 he went to Syria and [[damascus]] [@sep-al-farabi, preamble]. He may have travelled to Egypt, and he died in Damascus in December 950 or January 951 [@sep-al-farabi, preamble]. His ethnic origin is disputed. Some scholars made him a Turk, and more recent research points to a Persian [@sep-al-farabi, preamble].

His two main interests were philosophy, logic above all, and music [@sep-al-farabi, preamble]. He is known as "the second master", the first being [[aristotle]] [@sep-al-farabi, preamble]. His Great Book of Music is the most important medieval treatise on music from Islamic lands, and it has philosophical sections of its own [@sep-al-farabi, preamble].

## Aristotle and the Sciences

His Enumeration of the Sciences sets out the Aristotelian sciences in their traditional order, and its medieval Latin versions had much influence in the West [@sep-al-farabi, §1]. He knew a great many of Aristotle's works, some of the zoological treatises among them [@sep-al-farabi, §1]. The Stanford article on Greek sources in Arabic says that the full Aristotelian corpus, available in Arabic together with Euclid, Ptolemy, Hippocrates and Galen, let him plan a curriculum of higher education that took the native Islamic sciences into the wider system of philosophical sciences [@sep-arabic-islamic-greek, preamble].

In logic he separated demonstration, which alone is philosophy in the strict sense, from the dialectic of the theologians and from the rhetoric and poetry that scripture uses to reach ordinary people [@sep-al-farabi, §3]. On future contingents he took up Aristotle's problem from On Interpretation, added God's foreknowledge to it, and defended human free will against some theologians [@sep-al-farabi, §3]. Following Aristotle, he held that the world had no beginning and that the heavenly spheres have been moving from all eternity [@sep-al-ghazali, §2].

## Metaphysics and the One

In the Enumeration of the Sciences he divides metaphysics into three parts. The third deals with beings that are not bodies and are not in bodies, and shows them ranked in a hierarchy that rises to the First, or One [@sep-arabic-islamic-greek, §4]. On Thérèse-Anne Druart's reading, quoted in the same article, he abandoned [[al-kindi]]'s placing of the One above being and above intellect and gave the Neoplatonic One some features of Aristotle's Prime Mover [@sep-arabic-islamic-greek, §4]. He kept the First, or God, distinct from the Agent Intellect, and because the First knows only itself, emanation follows necessarily and gives rise to the world eternally [@sep-arabic-islamic-greek, §4].

His short Aims of Aristotle's Metaphysics says that metaphysics is not a theological science and studies what all beings share, such as being and unity [@sep-al-farabi, §6]. Two other works, The Political Regime and the Opinions of the People of the Perfect City, take another line. Each opens with a metaphysical part laid out as a Neo-Platonic descent and then turns to the organization of the state, whose hierarchy mirrors the hierarchy of emanation [@sep-al-farabi, §6]. Whether that descent grounds his political philosophy, or is a rhetorical appeal to make his views acceptable to religious authorities and ordinary people as the Straussians claim, is still debated [@sep-al-farabi, §6]. Dimitri Gutas, Stephen Menn and Druart take the Neo-Platonic descent to be central to his work [@sep-al-farabi, §6].

The Stanford article on Neoplatonism names al-Farabi, with al-Kindi and Ibn Sina, as one of the thinkers through whom Neoplatonic thought, by way of the [[theology-of-aristotle]], helped bring ancient philosophy and science into Islam [@sep-neoplatonism, §8]. The Encyclopedia of Religion calls the philosophy of al-Farabi and Ibn Sina "Arabic Neoplatonism" [@eor-ghazali, preamble]. The Harmony of the Two Sages, a work ascribed to him, cites the Theology to show that Plato and Aristotle agree on the a-temporal creation of the universe and on the Forms [@sep-theology-aristotle, §5]. Under that title it includes propositions from the Arabic Proclus, so the Theology it has in mind is a larger work than the one that survives [@sep-theology-aristotle, §5]. The Harmony's authenticity is disputed. Marwan Rashed put it in serious doubt in 2009, while Cecilia Martini Bonadeo argued for al-Farabi's authorship in her 2008 edition [@sep-al-farabi, §7]. The question bears on how his Aristotelianism relates to his Neo-Platonism [@sep-al-farabi, §7].

## Plato and the State

For his political philosophy he drew not on Aristotle's Politics, which seems never to have reached Arabic, but on [[plato]]'s [[republic]] and Laws [@sep-al-farabi, §7]. His access to both texts may have been limited, since it is doubtful that a full Arabic translation of either ever existed [@sep-al-farabi, §7]. His Summary of Plato's Laws is very brief and covers only the first eight books, and his own Philosophy of Plato shows no detailed knowledge of Plato's works [@sep-al-farabi, §7].

He changed what he took from Plato to suit a different world. In place of a city-state with one language and one people, he pictured a large empire of many cultures, languages and religions, and he turned the philosopher king into a philosopher prophet ruler [@sep-al-farabi, §7]. In his account, prophets and the religions they bring express what philosophers teach, but they use symbols to make that wisdom accessible to ordinary people [@sep-al-ghazali, §2].

He wrote a commentary on Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics that is now lost [@sep-al-farabi, §7]. Ibn Bajja, Ibn Tufayl and Averroes report that in it he denied the immortality of the human soul and any conjunction with the Agent Intellect, although other works of his, the Opinions among them, call that conjunction possible and the highest happiness [@sep-al-farabi, §7].

## Readers

[[ibn-sina]] continued al-Farabi's approach and developed his metaphysics and his account of prophecy [@sep-al-ghazali, §2]. The educational syllabus of the philosopher-king that al-Farabi set out lies in the background of Ibn Sina's program [@sep-arabic-islamic-greek, preamble].

According to the Stanford article on [[al-ghazali]], the first and longest of the twenty discussions in the [[incoherence-of-the-philosophers]] takes up the arguments of Ibn Sina and al-Farabi for the world's pre-eternity [@sep-al-ghazali, §3]. Al-Ghazali denies that this position can be proven by demonstration, and he argues that an eternal world is impossible [@sep-al-ghazali, §3].

[[ibn-rushd]]'s long commentary on the Posterior Analytics engages critically and severely with al-Farabi and Ibn Sina, the two Islamic authorities in logic before him [@sep-ibn-rushd, §2]. The Stanford article on Ibn Rushd traces to al-Farabi's influence his treatment of dialectic as a method with its own rules [@sep-ibn-rushd, §2].
