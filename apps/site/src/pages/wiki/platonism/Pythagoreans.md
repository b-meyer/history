# Pythagoreans

The name Pythagoreans covers several groups. It can mean the followers of [[pythagoras]] himself, the fifth- and fourth-century thinkers whom Aristotle called "the so-called Pythagoreans", and the later revivalists known as [[neopythagoreans]]. [@sep-pythagoreanism, preamble] This page follows the first two, from the community at [[croton]] to the school at [[tarentum]].

## The Community at Croton

Diogenes Laertius says that Pythagoras sailed to Croton, laid down a constitution for the Italian Greeks, and that he and his followers, nearly three hundred in number, governed the state so well that its constitution was in effect an aristocracy. [@diogenes-laertius-lives-hicks, DL 8.3] The modern account is more cautious. It dates his arrival to about 530 BCE and says only that Croton is where he first seems to have attracted a large number of followers. [@sep-pythagoras, §3]

An older history of mathematics gives the traditional picture of the inner circle. Those admitted to the teaching formed a brotherhood with all things in common, bound by oath not to reveal the school's secrets. [@rouse-ball-short-account-history-mathematics, p. 15] The same history says the strict discipline and secret organisation brought the society the hatred of various classes, and that a mob finally killed Pythagoras and many of his followers. [@rouse-ball-short-account-history-mathematics, p. 16] The better evidence, from Aristoxenus and Dicaearchus, is that violence against Pythagoras and his followers at Croton around 510 BCE, perhaps provoked by the exclusive nature of their way of life, drove him to flee to [[metapontum]]. [@sep-pythagoras, §3]

## Attacks and Dispersal

Outsiders attacked the Pythagorean societies again around 450 BCE. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §3.4] Plutarch tells that Philolaus, then a young man, was one of two who escaped when the Pythagorean meeting place at Metapontum was burned in 454. Earlier versions of the story do not name him, so his part in it is far from certain. [@sep-philolaus, §1.1]

After the attacks the school did not simply end. Rouse Ball says that the Pythagoreans seem to have re-established themselves at once as a society devoted to philosophy and mathematics, headquartered at Tarentum, and to have flourished for over a century. [@rouse-ball-short-account-history-mathematics, p. 16] The Stanford Encyclopedia judges the communities that kept a way of life they ascribed to Pythagoras likely to have vanished by about the middle of the fourth century BCE. [@sep-pythagoreanism, preamble] It adds that Pythagoreanism appears to have largely died out for the two centuries after the death of Aristoxenus. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §3.1]

## Acusmatici and Mathematici

Later writers describe a split inside the school. The acusmatici stressed rules for living, including taboos, while the mathematici stressed mathematics and natural philosophy. Each claimed to be the true Pythagoreans. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §3.4] The account comes from Iamblichus, who gives two contradictory versions. The scholarly consensus accepts the split on the strength of Burkert's argument that Iamblichus drew on Aristotle, though Zhmud holds that the split was invented by the later tradition. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §3.4]

Diogenes lists Hippasus of Metapontum as a Pythagorean who held that the changes of the universe take a definite time to complete, and reports, on the authority of Demetrius, that he left nothing in writing. [@diogenes-laertius-lives-hicks, DL 8.84] The modern account treats him as the first Pythagorean who can be identified with some confidence as a natural philosopher, mathematician and music theorist, and has him disavowed by both sides of the split. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §3.4]

## Philolaus

Philolaus came from Croton, in southern Italy, and lived from about 470 to about 385 BCE. He wrote one book, On Nature, probably the first by a Pythagorean. [@sep-philolaus, preamble] Diogenes counts him as a Pythagorean. [@diogenes-laertius-lives-hicks, DL 8.84] His city is disputed. Diogenes and the pupil of Aristotle named Meno say Croton, while Aristoxenus says Tarentum. One proposed reconciliation has him born at Croton and settled at Tarentum late in life. [@sep-philolaus, §1.1]

In his book the cosmos and everything in it are made of limiters and unlimiteds, fitted together in a "harmonia" that can be described in numbers. [@sep-philolaus, preamble] Not everyone labels him simply a Pythagorean. Neither Plato nor Meno applies the label to him, and the modern account stresses that he was as much a Presocratic philosopher as a member of the school. [@sep-philolaus, §1.1]

## Archytas

Archytas of Tarentum was active in the first half of the fourth century BCE. He was the final prominent figure of the early Pythagorean tradition and led the politics of his city, where he was elected general seven times in succession. [@sep-archytas, preamble] Diogenes also calls him a Pythagorean and says that the law excluded everyone else from even a second year of command. [@diogenes-laertius-lives-hicks, DL 8.79] An older history places him around 400 BCE among the most influential citizens of Tarentum. [@rouse-ball-short-account-history-mathematics, p. 22]

Cicero reports that Archytas was a pupil of Philolaus, which the Stanford Encyclopedia calls not improbable. [@sep-archytas, §1.1] The mathematician Eudoxus studied geometry with him. [@sep-archytas, §1.1] Rouse Ball calls Archytas the then head of the Pythagoreans when Eudoxus studied at Tarentum. [@rouse-ball-short-account-history-mathematics, p. 36] Archytas sent a ship in 361 to rescue Plato from Dionysius II, the tyrant of Syracuse. [@sep-archytas, preamble]

## Aristoxenus and the Last Generation

Aristoxenus, born at Tarentum, was a Pythagorean in his early years before he joined Aristotle's school. He wrote several works on Pythagoreanism that survive only in quotations, and he is one of the main sources for the early school. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §3.5] His teacher Xenophilus was a Pythagorean. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §3.5] The catalogue at the end of Iamblichus's On the Pythagorean Life lists 218 men and 17 women, and scholars debate how closely it reflects Aristoxenus. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §3.1] Women appear to have been more active in Pythagoreanism than in any other ancient philosophical movement. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §3.3]

## Plato and the Pythagoreans

Scholars disagree widely about how much Plato owed to the Pythagoreans. Some ancient and modern writers tie him closely to the school, and a biography of Pythagoras that Photius read in the ninth century makes him a pupil of Archytas and the ninth successor to Pythagoras. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §3.7] A more limited view holds that Plato names Pythagoras and the Pythagoreans only once each in the dialogues. It finds clear influence in the Philebus, where Plato acknowledges earlier thinkers who posit limit and unlimited as basic principles, and treats that as Pythagorean influence on Plato's late theory of principles. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §3.7]

Plato first visited southern Italy and Sicily in 388 or 387 BCE, and met Archytas there. [@sep-archytas, §1.4] The sources give conflicting pictures of the relationship, and the Stanford Encyclopedia concludes that Plato sought out Archytas as an expert in the mathematical sciences, not as a master philosopher. [@sep-archytas, §1.4] A report that Plato met an aged Philolaus at Tarentum on that trip is a possibility only. [@sep-philolaus, §7.2] For Plato himself, see [[plato]].

## After the Fourth Century

The last form of the tradition, Neopythagoreanism, is a tendency to treat Pythagoras as the central and original figure of Greek philosophy. It probably began among Plato's first successors in the Academy and flourished from the first century BCE. [@sep-pythagoreanism, preamble] It is the subject of [[neopythagoreans]].
