# Plotinus

Plotinus (204/5 to 270) was a Platonist philosopher who taught in Rome, and he is commonly regarded as the founder of [[neoplatonists|Neoplatonism]]. [@sep-neoplatonism, §1] The label is a modern one, since Neoplatonism was never the name of an ancient school. [@sep-iamblichus, §2] His treatises survive as the [[enneads]], which his pupil [[porphyry]] arranged. [@porphyry-life-of-plotinus-mackenna, p. 24]

## Life

Porphyry, who wrote the [[life-of-plotinus]], says that Plotinus could never be induced to speak of his ancestry, his parents or his birthplace. [@porphyry-life-of-plotinus-mackenna, ch. I] Eunapius calls him an Egyptian and names his native place as Lyco, while noting that Porphyry did not record it. [@eunapius-lives-wright, p. 353] Porphyry says Plotinus was sixty-six when he died in the second year of Claudius. [@porphyry-life-of-plotinus-mackenna, p. 2]

According to Porphyry, Plotinus was discouraged by the teachers he first heard at [[alexandria]] until a friend suggested [[ammonius-saccas]]. [@porphyry-life-of-plotinus-mackenna, p. 3] He followed Ammonius for eleven years, then joined the emperor Gordian's expedition against Persia, and after Gordian was killed he made his way back to Antioch with difficulty. Porphyry has him settle in [[rome]] at forty, in the reign of Philip. [@porphyry-life-of-plotinus-mackenna, p. 3] The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy dates the move to late 244 and counts more than twenty-five years of continuous teaching there until his death in 270. [@sep-plotinus, §1]

## Teaching in Rome

For his first ten years in Rome Plotinus wrote nothing. He held conferences with a circle of associates and based them on what he had learned from Ammonius. About the first year of Gallienus he began to write on subjects that had come up in the conferences. [@porphyry-life-of-plotinus-mackenna, p. 4] [[amelius]], one of the circle, had transcribed and arranged nearly all of the works of [[numenius]]. [@porphyry-life-of-plotinus-mackenna, pp. 4-5] Porphyry came from Greece in the tenth year of Gallienus and stayed about six years. [@porphyry-life-of-plotinus-mackenna, pp. 4-5]

Porphyry says that Gallienus and his wife Salonina honored Plotinus, and that he asked the emperor to rebuild a ruined city of philosophers in [[campania]], to be called Platonopolis and governed by Plato's laws. Opposition at court ended the plan. [@porphyry-life-of-plotinus-mackenna, p. 12]

When his health failed, Plotinus left Rome for Campania. He died there at the end of the second year of Claudius, according to Eustochius, the one friend at his bedside. [@porphyry-life-of-plotinus-mackenna, p. 2] Eustochius reported that Plotinus's last words were that he was striving to give back the Divine in himself to the Divine in the All. [@porphyry-life-of-plotinus-mackenna, p. 2]

## Writings

Porphyry lists a treatise On Numbers among them. [@porphyry-life-of-plotinus-mackenna, p. 6] The treatises circulated in a small circle and carried no titles from their author. [@porphyry-life-of-plotinus-mackenna, p. 4]

Porphyry edited them after Plotinus's death. He divided fifty-four treatises into six sets of nine, and he says he liked pairing six, the perfect number, with nines. [@porphyry-life-of-plotinus-mackenna, p. 24] Number in Porphyry's arrangement is taken up again in [Number Mysticism from Pythagoras to Proclus](platonism/Number-Mysticism.md).

Porphyry also says that Stoic and Peripatetic doctrines are buried in the treatises, and that Aristotle's Metaphysics is condensed in them almost entire. [@porphyry-life-of-plotinus-mackenna, p. 13]

## Thought

Plotinus drew mainly on principles he took to be Plato's. [@sep-plotinus, preamble] Some Greeks of his day accused him of borrowing from Numenius, and Amelius wrote a reply. [@porphyry-life-of-plotinus-mackenna, p. 14] The Stanford Encyclopedia entry on Numenius says his work shows how much of Plotinus's metaphysics earlier Platonists such as Numenius inspired. [@sep-numenius, preamble]

The Stanford Encyclopedia says the first principle has to be completely uncompounded, the absolute One, because a cause must be simpler than its effect. No predicate can be properly applied to it, and it is identified with the Good. [@sep-plotinus, §7] The same entry says that cathartic practices let people develop abilities that allow an experiential union or contact with it, although direct intellective apprehension of it is impossible. [@sep-plotinus, §7]

Below the One is Intellect, the domain of the intelligible Forms. The Forms are active intellects, each containing all the others in potential, so that Intellect is a unity made of many. [@sep-plotinus, §6] The Forms are ordered by eidetic numbers. [@sep-plotinus, §6] Soul is present as a whole in every part of the body it animates. [@sep-plotinus, §4]
