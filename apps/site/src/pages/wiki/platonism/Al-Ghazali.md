# al-Ghazali

Abu Hamid al-Ghazali (1058 to 1111) was an Islamic jurist, theologian and mystic. [@eor-ghazali, preamble] In [[incoherence-of-the-philosophers]] he criticized "twenty positions of falsafa", the Arabic tradition of Aristotelian philosophy. [@sep-al-ghazali, preamble]

## Life

Al-Ghazali was born at [[tus]], near modern Mashhad in eastern Iran, and had his first schooling there. [@eor-ghazali, preamble] The Encyclopedia of Religion puts his birth in 1058. [@eor-sufism, preamble] The Stanford Encyclopedia reports that later Muslim historians give 1058 or 1059, while remarks on his age in his letters and autobiography point to 1055 or 1056. [@sep-al-ghazali, §1]

He studied at the Nizamiyya college in [[nishapur]] under the Ashʿarite theologian al-Juwayni. [@sep-al-ghazali, §1] In 1091 he was appointed to the main professorship at the Nizamiyya college in [[baghdad]], at about thirty-three. [@eor-ghazali, preamble] The appointment came from the vizier Nizam al-Mulk. [@eor-sufism, preamble]

In 1095 an inner crisis brought on an illness that left him unable to lecture, and after about six months he gave up the professorship to live as a Sufi. [@eor-ghazali, preamble] The Stanford Encyclopedia says he had already begun to change his way of life two years before he left, "under the influence of Sufi literature". [@sep-al-ghazali, §1] He spent more than a year in [[damascus]] living as a Sufi, and in 1096 he made the pilgrimage to [[mecca]]. [@eor-ghazali, preamble] He then seems to have made his way back to Tus in stages, and there he founded a khanaqah, a convent where young disciples joined him in a shared Sufi life. [@eor-ghazali, preamble]

Some Muslim scholars have doubted that the conversion was genuine and suggested that he left Baghdad because political ties put his life in danger. [@eor-ghazali, preamble] The Encyclopedia of Religion judges from his own account that religious reasons came first. [@eor-ghazali, preamble]

The Muslim year 500, which began in September 1106, opened a new century, and friends told al-Ghazali he was the "renewer" promised for it. [@eor-ghazali, preamble] He accepted the main professorship at the Nizamiyya in Nishapur. [@eor-ghazali, preamble] The Stanford Encyclopedia notes that this broke a vow, sworn at Abraham's tomb in Hebron, to stay out of the service of rulers and away from state-funded schools. [@sep-al-ghazali, §1] He held the post for "three or possibly four years", returned to Tus, probably in poor health, and died there in 1111. [@eor-ghazali, preamble]

## Sufism

In his autobiography al-Ghazali writes that Sufi literature taught him that beliefs alone do not secure the afterlife and that only good actions do, and this led him to take up the Sufi path. [@sep-al-ghazali, §5] He began his major work, The Revival of the Religious Sciences, in the period after he left Baghdad. [@sep-al-ghazali, §5] It has four sections of ten books each, on ritual practice, social custom, what leads to perdition and what leads to salvation. [@sep-al-ghazali, §5]

The Encyclopedia of Religion describes his position as that of the scholars who practised Sufism in moderation, without heresy in doctrine or neglect of the religious law. [@eor-ghazali, preamble] After his life and work, mainstream Sunni scholars had to view the Sufi movement more favourably, and ordinary Muslims could take up moderate Sufi practice. [@eor-ghazali, preamble]

## The Philosophers

The Arabic philosophers, the falasifa, inherited the late-antique habit of reading Aristotle in Neoplatonic terms. [@sep-al-ghazali, §2] Some plainly Neoplatonic texts reached Arabic, among them [[theology-of-aristotle]], compiled from the [[enneads]] of [[plotinus]], but the most significant Neoplatonic contributions came through commentaries on Aristotle. [@sep-al-ghazali, §2] Following Aristotle, [[al-farabi]] taught that the world has no beginning in the past, and [[ibn-sina]] carried on his approach. [@sep-al-ghazali, §2] The Encyclopedia of Religion calls the philosophy al-Ghazali studied "the Arabic Neoplatonism of al-Fārābi and Ibn Sīnā". [@eor-ghazali, preamble]

In his autobiography al-Ghazali says that at the Baghdad Nizamiyya he studied the philosophers' works for two years and wrote the Incoherence in a third. [@sep-al-ghazali, §2] The Stanford Encyclopedia treats this account as apologetic and thinks he probably first met falsafa as al-Juwayni's student, since al-Juwayni's works already show Ibn Sina's influence. [@sep-al-ghazali, §2] It dates his books answering falsafa to around 1095. [@sep-al-ghazali, §2]

His Doctrines of the Philosophers is a loose Arabic adaptation of the logic, metaphysics and natural science in Ibn Sina's Persian Philosophy for ʿAla al-Dawla. [@sep-al-ghazali, §2] The Latin translation left out the passages in which al-Ghazali calls the work a report, and Latin readers took its author "Algazel" for a faithful follower of Avicenna. [@sep-al-ghazali, §2]

## The Incoherence of the Philosophers

Al-Ghazali examines twenty teachings of the falasifa in the Incoherence, sixteen from their metaphysics and four from their natural science. [@sep-al-ghazali, §3] He argues that none of the arguments for them meets the standard of demonstration the philosophers set for themselves. [@sep-al-ghazali, §3] The first and longest discussion deals with Ibn Sina's and al-Farabi's arguments that the world is pre-eternal. [@sep-al-ghazali, §3] He goes further there and argues that an eternal world is impossible, using arguments developed earlier by the Christian John Philoponus of Alexandria. [@sep-al-ghazali, §3]

At the end of the book he judges most of the twenty positions to be wrong without being a danger to religion. [@sep-al-ghazali, §3] Three teachings of Ibn Sina are worse. They are that the world has no beginning in time, that God knows only universals and not particulars, and that souls never return to bodies after death. [@sep-al-ghazali, §3] A short fatwa at the end declares anyone who teaches these three in public an unbeliever. [@sep-al-ghazali, §3]

The Stanford Encyclopedia notes that the West long read the book through Ibn Rushd's reply as an anti-philosophical tract, and that its philosophical value has been recognized only in the 21st century. [@sep-al-ghazali, §3]

## Aristotle and Ibn Sina

Al-Ghazali held that each teaching of the philosophers should be judged on its own and adopted if it is sound and agrees with revelation. [@sep-al-ghazali, §4] The Stanford Encyclopedia finds that this attitude led him to apply Aristotelian teachings widely in his theology and ethics. [@sep-al-ghazali, §4] The Encyclopedia of Religion notes that he was "very impressed by Aristotelian logic", used it in defending doctrine and wrote several books on it. [@eor-ghazali, preamble]

Against the Ismaʿili claim that no rational proof is better than any other, he brought in the Aristotelian notion of demonstration. [@sep-al-ghazali, §4] If a valid demonstration contradicts the literal sense of revelation, the passage is to be read as a symbol, and where no demonstration does so, interpretation is not allowed. [@sep-al-ghazali, §4]

He also took ideas from Ibn Sina. He brought Avicennan psychology and the rational account of prophecy into Ashʿarite theology. [@sep-al-ghazali, §5] He followed Ibn Sina in holding that this universe is the best of all possible worlds, but against him he held that God chooses freely when He creates. [@sep-al-ghazali, §6]

## Ibn Rushd's Reply

[[ibn-rushd]] accepted al-Ghazali's rule for reconciling reason with the outward meaning of revelation but disagreed with his findings on what can be demonstrated. [@sep-al-ghazali, §4] He wrote a refutation of the Incoherence, [[incoherence-of-the-incoherence]]. [@sep-al-ghazali, §4] He held that Aristotle had already demonstrated the world's pre-eternity, which by al-Ghazali's own rule would make it a religious doctrine as well. [@sep-al-ghazali, §4] The reply was translated into Latin in 1328 and 1526 as Destructio destructionum, and through it Renaissance Averroists came to know al-Ghazali's criticism of Aristotelianism. [@sep-al-ghazali, §4]

The Encyclopedia of Religion notes that no philosopher of weight had worked in the East since Ibn Sina died, twenty years before al-Ghazali was born. [@eor-ghazali, preamble] Philosophy in the Greek tradition went on in the Islamic West until about 1200, and Ibn Rushd's refutation belongs to it. [@eor-ghazali, preamble]
