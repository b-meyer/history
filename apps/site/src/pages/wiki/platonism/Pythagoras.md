# Pythagoras

Pythagoras lived from about 570 to about 490 BCE. He spent his early years on [[samos]], emigrated to [[croton]] in southern Italy at the age of forty, and did most of his philosophical work there. [@sep-pythagoras, preamble] He wrote nothing, and no contemporary left a detailed account of his thought. [@sep-pythagoras, preamble]

## The Pythagorean Question

By the third century CE, when the first detailed accounts that survive intact were written, some circles treated him as the master philosopher and the source of Greek philosophical truth. [@sep-pythagoras, §1] Late in the first century BCE a large collection of books was being passed off as the work of Pythagoras and other early Pythagoreans. [@sep-pythagoras, §1]

The three fullest ancient lives, by Diogenes Laertius, Porphyry and Iamblichus, date to the third century CE, some 800 years after his death. [@sep-pythagoras, §2.2] Iamblichus wrote [[on-the-pythagorean-way-of-life]], which includes some biography but concentrates on the way of life Pythagoras set up for his followers. [@sep-pythagoras, §2.2] This page therefore reports what the later authors say as their account, and marks where modern scholars judge it unreliable.

## Life

Diogenes Laertius gives two origins for him. Hermippus calls him a Samian, and Aristoxenus calls him a Tyrrhenian. [@diogenes-laertius-lives-hicks, DL 8.1] Aristoxenus also says that Pythagoras left Samos at forty, when the tyranny of Polycrates became unbearable, which would put his birth around 570 BCE. [@sep-pythagoras, §3]

Diogenes says that Pythagoras returned to Samos, found his country under Polycrates, and sailed to Croton, where he laid down a constitution for the Italian Greeks. [@diogenes-laertius-lives-hicks, DL 8.3] The modern account dates the move to about 530 BCE and says that Croton is where he first seems to have attracted a large number of followers. [@sep-pythagoras, §3] Iamblichus tells that on his arrival he had many followers, "six hundred" by one count. [@iamblichus-pythagoric-life-taylor, ch. V] The community that formed around him is the subject of [[pythagoreans]].

The accounts of his end disagree. In one story in Diogenes, the house of Milo, where he sat among his acquaintances, was set on fire. [@diogenes-laertius-lives-hicks, DL 8.39] Diogenes also reports Dicaearchus saying that Pythagoras died at [[metapontum]], a fugitive in the temple of the Muses. [@diogenes-laertius-lives-hicks, DL 8.40] Iamblichus says that writers differ on whether he went to Pherecydes the Syrian or to Metapontum when the attack came. [@iamblichus-pythagoric-life-taylor, ch. XXXV] Modern scholars take the most reliable evidence, from Aristoxenus and Dicaearchus, to be that violence against Pythagoras and his followers at Croton around 510 BCE drove him to Metapontum, where he died around 490 BCE. [@sep-pythagoras, §3]

## Writings

Diogenes Laertius says that Pythagoras wrote three books, On Education, On Statesmanship and On Nature. [@diogenes-laertius-lives-hicks, DL 8.6] The modern account differs, and holds that he wrote nothing. [@sep-pythagoras, preamble] The poem called the [[golden-verses]], 71 hexameters, was ascribed to Pythagoras or the Pythagoreans, but it combines material of different dates, and suggested dates for its present form run from 350 BCE to 400 CE. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.5]

## Teaching and Reputation

The earliest evidence shows that Pythagoras was known above all as an expert on the fate of the soul after death. [@sep-pythagoras, §4.1] The same early evidence includes stories of his superhuman nature, among them a golden thigh and an appearance on one day in both Metapontum and Croton. [@sep-pythagoras, §4.2] He was also famous for a way of life that still had adherents in the fourth century, though nothing in the early evidence ties that way of life to reincarnation in any specific fashion. [@sep-pythagoras, §4.3]

In the modern world he is best known as a mathematician and cosmologist, but the early evidence gives no sign that his fame rested mainly on mathematics or cosmology. [@sep-pythagoras, §5] The story that he coined the word "philosophy" is one of the later attempts to glorify him. It is first found in Heraclides of Pontus, in the early Academy. [@sep-pythagoras, §4]
