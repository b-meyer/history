# Plato

Plato was the son of Ariston and a citizen of [[athens]] [@diogenes-laertius-lives-hicks, DL 3.1]. Diogenes Laertius reports that Apollodorus placed his birth in the 88th Olympiad and that, according to Hermippus, he died at a wedding feast in his eighty-first year [@diogenes-laertius-lives-hicks, DL 3.2]. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy dates his life 429?-347 BCE [@sep-plato, preamble]. His birthplace is disputed, since some said he was born on Aegina [@diogenes-laertius-lives-hicks, DL 3.3].

## Life

The ancient lives say Plato became a pupil of [[socrates]] at about twenty. After Socrates was gone he is said to have turned to Cratylus the Heraclitean and to Hermogenes, then to have travelled to [[megara]], [[cyrene]], Italy and [[egypt]] [@diogenes-laertius-lives-hicks, DL 3.6]. The same passage names the Pythagoreans Philolaus and Eurytus as the people he went to Italy to see [@diogenes-laertius-lives-hicks, DL 3.6]. Rouse Ball gives a different order and says that in Italy Plato became close to Archytas, Eurytus of Metapontum and Timaeus of Locri [@rouse-ball-short-account-history-mathematics, p. 35]. A modern reading dates his first visit to southern Italy and Sicily to 388/7, after the death of Socrates [@sep-archytas, §1.4]. Whether he met an aged Philolaus at [[tarentum]] on that trip is only a possibility [@sep-philolaus, §7.2], and Rouse Ball adds that Eudoxus also went to Tarentum and studied under Archytas [@rouse-ball-short-account-history-mathematics, p. 36].

Diogenes says Plato made three voyages to Sicily. On the first, the elder Dionysius took offence at Plato's views on tyranny [@diogenes-laertius-lives-hicks, DL 3.18]. On a later voyage, to the younger Dionysius, some say Plato was in danger as a suspected backer of a plan to free the island, and Archytas the Pythagorean wrote to Dionysius and got him home safe to Athens [@diogenes-laertius-lives-hicks, DL 3.21]. The Stanford article on Archytas dates the rescue to 361, when Archytas sent a ship to take Plato from Dionysius II of [[syracuse]] [@sep-archytas, preamble]. The same article describes the personal and philosophical ties between the two men as complex and the evidence for disagreement as plentiful [@sep-archytas, preamble].

Plato lived in the Academy, a gymnasium outside the walls of Athens, after he returned [@diogenes-laertius-lives-hicks, DL 3.7]. Rouse Ball puts his return at about 380 BCE and says he formed a school there [@rouse-ball-short-account-history-mathematics, p. 35]. He was buried in the Academy, and the school he founded was called the Academic school [@diogenes-laertius-lives-hicks, DL 3.41]. The [[academy]] has its own article.

## Writings

Nearly everything Plato wrote is a dialogue, with the Apology as the one striking exception, and the thirteen letters transmitted under his name are not all accepted as his [@sep-plato, §3]. Socrates speaks in nearly every dialogue and is absent only from the Laws [@sep-plato, §4]. Diogenes lists the [[republic]], [[timaeus]] and Critias together in the eighth of his groups of four [@diogenes-laertius-lives-hicks, DL 3.60]. He also reports that the opening of the Republic was found revised several times [@diogenes-laertius-lives-hicks, DL 3.37].

The Republic takes up who should rule. Jowett's analysis of Book VI has Socrates ask whether philosophers or the many should govern, and answer that philosophers should, if they have the other qualities a ruler needs, because they love knowledge of the eternal and of all truth [@plato-republic-jowett, preamble]. In the Timaeus, Socrates presents Timaeus as a man of Locris in Italy [@plato-timaeus-jowett, preamble]. Socrates has a small, peripheral role in that dialogue, and in the Timaeus and Critias another figure gives a long, continuous discourse [@sep-plato, §5].

## Doctrine

The Stanford article names as the central distinction the one between the many observable things that appear beautiful and the one object that is what beauty really is [@sep-plato, §1]. On this reading Plato's works urge the reader to value the forms over the defective bodily world, and the soul is a different sort of object from the body, able to grasp the forms more easily when free of anything corporeal [@sep-plato, §1].

Attributing a doctrine to Plato is itself debated. He affirms nothing in his own voice in any dialogue, so some ask whether his convictions can be recovered at all [@sep-plato, §6]. The Stanford article argues that dialogues in which one character dominates the conversation were written to bring readers to certain conclusions, and that Plato probably meant all his writings as aids to conversation [@sep-plato, §6].

## Pythagorean Influence

Diogenes says Plato mixed the doctrines of Heraclitus, the Pythagoreans and Socrates, agreeing with Heraclitus about sensible things, with Pythagoras about the intelligible, and with Socrates in political philosophy [@diogenes-laertius-lives-hicks, DL 3.8]. Rouse Ball suggests that his philosophy may be better seen as founded on Pythagorean than on Socratic teaching, since it shared the idea that the secret of the universe lies in number and form [@rouse-ball-short-account-history-mathematics, p. 35]. Rouse Ball also gives the inscription said to stand over the school's door, which barred anyone ignorant of geometry [@rouse-ball-short-account-history-mathematics, p. 35].

Scholars hold a very wide range of views on the question. The Stanford article on Pythagoreanism says the evidence of Plato and Aristotle together suggests considerable Pythagorean influence on Plato's late theory of principles, and that the world soul of the Timaeus is built on the diatonic scale prominent in [[philolaus]] [@sep-pythagoreanism, §3.7]. It adds that most of the Timaeus does not come from Pythagoreanism, and that [[archytas]] argued for an unlimited universe where Plato's is limited [@sep-pythagoreanism, §3.7]. See also [[pythagoreans]].
