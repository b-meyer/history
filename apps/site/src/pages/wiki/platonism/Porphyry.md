# Porphyry

Porphyry (probably 234 to about 305) was a Neoplatonist philosopher born at [[tyre]] in Phoenicia. [@sep-porphyry, preamble] He was the student and editor of [[plotinus]], and he arranged the [[enneads]] in the form in which they have been read since. [@sep-porphyry, preamble]

## Life

Porphyry was born at Tyre, probably in 234. [@sep-porphyry, §1] Eunapius also names Tyre as his birthplace. [@eunapius-lives-wright, p. 353] His native name was Malchos, "king" in his native tongue, which he rendered in Greek as Basileus. [@sep-porphyry, §1] Eunapius says that [[longinus]] gave him the name Porphyry, [@eunapius-lives-wright, p. 355] The Stanford Encyclopedia notes that no other source supports this. [@sep-porphyry, §1]

He studied at [[athens]] with the Middle Platonist Longinus. [@sep-porphyry, §1] Eunapius also makes him a pupil of Longinus. [@eunapius-lives-wright, p. 353] In 263 he moved to [[rome]] and stayed about five years. [@sep-porphyry, §1] Porphyry himself says that he arrived from Greece in the tenth year of Gallienus, [@porphyry-life-of-plotinus-mackenna, p. 4] and that he spent six years in close relation with Plotinus. [@porphyry-life-of-plotinus-mackenna, p. 5]

Porphyry says that when he first heard Plotinus he tried to show that the intelligibles exist outside Intellect. [[amelius]] answered him, and after a further exchange Porphyry was persuaded, wrote a recantation and read it before the circle. [@porphyry-life-of-plotinus-mackenna, p. 17] The Stanford Encyclopedia notes that the status of the Ideas was the main point of doctrine dividing the schools of Longinus and Plotinus. [@sep-porphyry, §1]

Porphyry says that he once meant to end his life, and that Plotinus told him the plan came from melancholy and not from reason, and advised him to leave Rome. [@porphyry-life-of-plotinus-mackenna, p. 12] He left for Sicily in 268 and was still there when Plotinus died in 270. [@sep-porphyry, §1] He was at [[lilybaeum]] at the time of the death. [@porphyry-life-of-plotinus-mackenna, p. 2] Eunapius tells the story differently. In his version Porphyry, overcome by Plotinus's teaching, came to hate his own body and sailed to Sicily. [@eunapius-lives-wright, p. 355] Other scholars read the departure as political or as a doctrinal break with Plotinus, and the Stanford Encyclopedia finds the second reading hard to reconcile with Porphyry's later devotion to his master. [@sep-porphyry, §1]

The rest of his life is poorly known. [[carthage]] and Tyre are both attested as places he stayed, and he must have returned to Rome. [@sep-porphyry, §1] Eunapius reports that he is said to have died in Rome. [@eunapius-lives-wright, p. 361] The Stanford Encyclopedia dates the death to about 305. [@sep-porphyry, preamble]

## Editor of Plotinus

Porphyry says that Plotinus entrusted him with revising his writings. [@porphyry-life-of-plotinus-mackenna, p. 9] He divided the fifty-four treatises into six sets of nine, and he says he liked pairing the perfect number six with nines. [@porphyry-life-of-plotinus-mackenna, p. 24] The arrangement is taken up again in [Number Mysticism from Pythagoras to Proclus](platonism/Number-Mysticism.md).

He wrote the [[life-of-plotinus]] as a preface to the edition, probably in 301. [@sep-porphyry, §1] In it he says that Plotinus reached union with the highest God four times while Porphyry was with him, and that he himself was admitted once, in his sixty-eighth year. [@porphyry-life-of-plotinus-mackenna, p. 24] For the course of Plotinus's own life, see [Plotinus](platonism/Plotinus.md).

## Writings

Porphyry wrote on almost every branch of learning of his day, and only a small part survives. [@sep-porphyry, preamble] Goulet's catalogue lists more than sixty titles, most lost or fragmentary. [@sep-porphyry, §2] Among the works that survive are the Isagoge and the [[sententiae]], a digest of the Enneads that links Plotinus to Proclus's Elements of Theology. [@sep-porphyry, §2] His polemic Against the Christians is lost. [@sep-porphyry, preamble]

He was the first Platonist to comment on Aristotle, and he held that Plato and Aristotle agreed on essentials. [@sep-porphyry, preamble]

## Thought

Porphyry shares Plotinus's philosophy in broad outline, with the same three levels of One, Intellect and Soul. [@sep-porphyry, §3] He differs from Plotinus in a scholarly bent that Plotinus largely lacks, and in a greater interest in reconciling Aristotle with Platonism. [@sep-porphyry, §3] By applying Platonist doctrine to pagan religion, he defended philosophy, not ritual, as the way to the soul's salvation. [@sep-porphyry, preamble]

## Pythagoras and Iamblichus

Porphyry's [[life-of-pythagoras]] is the only large section of his Philosophical History to survive, and it is one of the main sources for Pythagoreanism. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.4] The Stanford Encyclopedia endorses O'Meara's description of him as a "universalizing Platonist" who found his Platonism in Pythagoras and in many other places, and notes that his own ascetic life was probably inspired by Pythagoreanism. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.4]

The Stanford Encyclopedia on Pythagoreanism calls [[iamblichus]] Porphyry's pupil and says he opposed his teacher on many points. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.4] The entry on Porphyry notes that the pupil relation rests on late sources and on literary links, since Iamblichus's [[de-mysteriis]] answers Porphyry's Letter to Anebo, and that Iamblichus was clearly strongly influenced by him all the same. [@sep-porphyry, §1]
