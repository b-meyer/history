# al-Kindi

Abu Yusuf Ya'qub ibn Ishaq al-Kindi (about 800 to 870) is described by the Stanford Encyclopedia as the first philosopher in the Arabic tradition to call himself one. [@sep-al-kindi, preamble] He worked with a group of translators who turned Greek philosophy and science into Arabic, including works of [[aristotle]] and the Neoplatonists. [@sep-al-kindi, preamble] His own thought was steeped in Neoplatonism, but in philosophical matters his main authority was Aristotle. [@sep-al-kindi, preamble]

## Life

Al-Kindi belonged to the Arab tribe of Kinda, and because of this lineage later writers called him the "philosopher of the Arabs". [@sep-al-kindi, §1.1] He died after 866, and his death is usually placed in the early 870s. [@sep-al-kindi, §1.1] His birth is harder to date, but he is reported to have served al-Ma'mun, caliph until 833, and he certainly belonged to the court of al-Mu'tasim (833 to 842), so scholars usually place his birth around 800. [@sep-al-kindi, §1.1] He was born in [[basra]] and educated in [[baghdad]]. [@sep-al-kindi, §1.1] His career peaked under al-Mu'tasim, to whom he dedicated On First Philosophy and whose son Ahmad he tutored. [@sep-al-kindi, §1.1] A survey of the Arabic translations adds that he addressed one of his works to al-Ma'mun. [@sep-arabic-islamic-greek, §3]

## The Kindi Circle

His work in philosophy centred on the translation movement that the Abbasid caliphs had sponsored since before he was born. [@sep-al-kindi, §1.1] In the ninth century he directed one of the two main translation groups, and Hunayn ibn Ishaq led the other. [@sep-al-kindi, §1.1] He seems to have stood between the patrons and the translators who did the work, many of whom were Syrian Christians or of Syrian descent. [@sep-al-kindi, §1.1]

The court library, the House of Wisdom, was not an institutional centre for translation, but the first group of translators had close ties to the court, and al-Kindi was its leader. [@sep-arabic-islamic-greek, §3] The early sources say the first Arabic Metaphysics of Aristotle was made for him, and the opening of the Theology of Aristotle names him as its reviser. [@sep-arabic-islamic-greek, §3] Because these two translations share their vocabulary and syntax with other translations from Greek, scholars take it that a circle of translators worked with him, and Endress has called him its "spiritus rector". [@sep-arabic-islamic-greek, §3] Among the authors the circle translated were [[plotinus]] and [[proclus]], with Plato, Aristotle, Alexander of Aphrodisias, Nicomachus of Gerasa, Olympiodorus and John Philoponus. [@sep-arabic-islamic-greek, §3] Plotinus went about under the name of Aristotle, and Proclus under the names of Aristotle and Alexander. [@sep-arabic-islamic-greek, §3]

The preface of the [[theology-of-aristotle]], an Arabic reworking of parts of the Enneads, says the text was translated by Ibn Na'ima al-Himsi and "corrected for Ahmad ibn al-Mu‘tasim by al-Kindi". [@sep-theology-aristotle, §2] Since Ahmad's father reigned from 833 to 842, the preface gives a fairly reliable date for the work, or at least for al-Kindi's correction of it. [@sep-theology-aristotle, §2] His share in it is disputed, since Zimmermann and Adamson think al-Himsi did most of the work and al-Kindi little, while D'Ancona has suggested he played a larger part. [@sep-theology-aristotle, §2] On Zimmermann's reading, which the Stanford entry finds plausible, the circle saw Plotinus as agreeing with Aristotle and completing him, without believing that Aristotle wrote the book. [@sep-theology-aristotle, §2] The circle set out to promote Greek philosophy and science and to make them Islamic, and it presented Greek thought as one coherent whole that bore on the questions of its own day. [@sep-theology-aristotle, §3] Zimmermann proposed that its Arabic Plotinus and Proclus, with works of Alexander, made up a single "metaphysics file". [@sep-theology-aristotle, §3]

## Greek Sources

His philosophy owes something to the mathematical and scientific authors translated in his time, such as Nicomachus of Gerasa, and Euclid shaped his method as well as his mathematics. [@sep-al-kindi, §2.1] Aristotle was the strongest influence on his philosophy, and al-Kindi surveyed Aristotle's writings in On the Quantity of Aristotle's Books, though he plainly had not read some of the treatises he describes. [@sep-al-kindi, §2.1] On First Philosophy draws on Aristotle's Metaphysics and also makes wide use of translated Neoplatonic writings. [@sep-al-kindi, §2.1]

Its proof of a "true One" rests in part on Proclus, as Jolivet showed in 1979. [@sep-al-kindi, §2.1] Endress describes the same argument as dependent "directly or indirectly on the Platonic Theology of Proclus". [@sep-arabic-islamic-greek, §3] The Stanford entry on al-Kindi says traces of the Theology of Aristotle can also be found in the work, and it names John Philoponus's attack on Aristotle over the world's eternity as perhaps the largest single influence. [@sep-al-kindi, §2.1] The Neoplatonists' own commentaries on Aristotle had already prepared the way for al-Kindi's habit of harmonizing Greek authors. [@sep-al-kindi, §2.1]

He wrote a short treatise against the Christian doctrine of the Trinity, which uses concepts from the Isagoge of [[porphyry]]. [@sep-al-kindi, §2.2] In On First Philosophy he follows the Isagoge in sorting all predicates into genus, species, difference, individual, proper accident and common accident. [@sep-al-kindi, §3.1] The Stanford entry on Neoplatonism names al-Kindi first, ahead of [[al-farabi]] and [[ibn-sina]], among those through whom the Theology of Aristotle carried Neoplatonic thought into Islam. [@sep-neoplatonism, §8]

## On First Philosophy

Only the first part of On First Philosophy survives, in four sections. [@sep-al-kindi, §1.2] The first urges the reader to honour Greek wisdom, the second discusses whether the world is eternal, and the third and fourth prove that a "true One", God, exists and consider why language cannot apply to it. [@sep-al-kindi, §1.2] Al-Kindi identifies first philosophy with knowledge of God, whom he calls "the first truth and the cause of all truth." [@sep-al-kindi, §3.1]

He argues that every kind of predicate implies both unity and multiplicity, and that their union in things needs a cause outside them that is one and in no way many. [@sep-al-kindi, §3.1] This cause is the true One, and none of the predicates applies to it. [@sep-al-kindi, §3.1] The Stanford entry finds that it closely resembles the first principle of the Neoplatonists. [@sep-al-kindi, §3.1] Because to be a thing is to be one in some way, the true One is also the cause of being, and al-Kindi calls God's act of bringing beings to be from non-being "origination". [@sep-al-kindi, §3.2] His main source for this view of creation is Philoponus, a pupil of Ammonius. [@sep-al-kindi, §3.2] His argument that the world is not eternal also follows Philoponus, and it turns Aristotle's denial of an actual infinite against Aristotle's own eternal world. [@sep-al-kindi, §3.3]

## Legacy

A line of students carried his approach through the tenth century, among them al-'Amiri, a Neoplatonist who studied under al-Kindi's pupil Abu Zayd al-Balkhi. [@sep-al-kindi, §6] Writers in Arabic after the tenth century seldom cite him, but Latin medieval authors read him, above all for his astrology. [@sep-al-kindi, §6] The translations of his circle, among them the Metaphysics and the Theology of Aristotle, remained standard texts for centuries. [@sep-al-kindi, §6] Al-Farabi never names him, and Averroes mentions him only to criticize his theory of drugs. [@sep-al-kindi, §6]
