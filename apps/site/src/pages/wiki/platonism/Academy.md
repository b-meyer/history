# Academy

The Academy was a gymnasium outside the walls of [[athens]], set in a grove named after a hero, Hecademus [@diogenes-laertius-lives-hicks, DL 3.7]. [[plato]] lived there after he came back to Athens [@diogenes-laertius-lives-hicks, DL 3.7]. MacTutor says he began to teach there around 387 BCE, so the opening date is approximate [@mactutor-academy, preamble]. Plato was buried in the Academy, and the school he founded was called the Academic school after it [@diogenes-laertius-lives-hicks, DL 3.41].

## What It Was

The modern word gives a false picture. MacTutor quotes Glucker, who says that in ancient Athens the Academy was first a public park dominated by its gymnasium, and that its link with Plato's school was one of many historical associations of the place [@mactutor-academy, preamble]. Cherniss, quoted in the same article, concludes that the school taught no orthodox metaphysical doctrine, that its members were not expected to subscribe to the theory of ideas, and that formal instruction was limited to mathematics [@mactutor-academy, preamble].

Diogenes Laertius lists Plato's disciples, beginning with Speusippus of Athens, Xenocrates of Chalcedon and Aristotle of Stagira, and adds two women, Lastheneia of Mantinea and Axiothea of Phlius [@diogenes-laertius-lives-hicks, DL 3.46]. The words said to stand over the door, barring anyone who was not a geometer, appear in no source before the fourth century CE, and MacTutor judges the story almost certainly fictitious [@mactutor-academy, preamble].

## The Early Heads

MacTutor says the head was elected for life by a majority vote, and names the first leaders as Plato, Speusippus, Xenocrates, Polemon, Crates and Crantor. Aristotle was a member for many years but never head [@mactutor-academy, preamble]. Speusippus was the son of Plato's sister Potone [@diogenes-laertius-lives-hicks, DL 3.4]. The Stanford article on Pythagoreanism calls him Plato's successor and says the evidence for him is fragmentary and second hand [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.1]. It reports that he wrote a book On Pythagorean Numbers, though it is not certain that the title is his [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.1]. His successor Xenocrates may have followed a version of the Pythagorean way of life, though Sedley distances him from Pythagoreanism [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.1].

The same article holds that [[neopythagoreans]] had their probable origin in the Academy of the later fourth century BCE, where Speusippus and Xenocrates are said to have presented speculations growing out of Plato's late metaphysics as the work of Pythagoras [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4]. The article notes that some scholars reject the evidence for Speusippus on this point, and that Zhmud disputes the reading of Xenocrates [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.1].

Arcesilaus became head in 265 BCE, and some writers date a New Academy from him, against the Old Academy of Plato to Crantor [@mactutor-academy, preamble].

## A Break and a Revival

Philo left Athens for Rome about 85 BCE, after Sulla's army besieged the city and many trees in the Academy park were cut for timber. MacTutor finds little evidence that the school was restarted before the second century CE [@mactutor-academy, preamble]. On that reading there was no Academy between 85 BCE and the second century CE, and the usual statement that the Academy ran from 387 BCE to 529 CE is inaccurate [@mactutor-academy, preamble].

## The Late School and 529

The Stanford article on Proclus calls the later Athenian school the Platonic "Academy" and says it was headed by a successor of Plato [@sep-proclus, preamble]. After Plutarch of Athens died in 432, Syrianus became head [@sep-proclus, §1.1]. [[proclus]] succeeded Syrianus after his death in 437 and held the post for almost fifty years, until his own death in 485 [@sep-proclus, §1.1].

The Stanford article on Neoplatonism says the group of Neoplatonists in Athens still keeping the old rituals disbanded after the legislation of Justinian in 529 [@sep-neoplatonism, §7]. That is the [[justinian-edict]], and the same article describes it as closing the Platonist academy [@sep-neoplatonism, §7].

MacTutor disputes that account. It says Damascius was head of the Academy in 529 and left Athens with Simplicius and other members, that Simplicius came back and led a much reduced Academy until his death in 560, and that the school therefore outlasted the edict [@mactutor-academy, preamble]. The closing date is therefore contested.
