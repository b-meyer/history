# Neopythagoreans

Neopythagoreanism is a tendency to make [[pythagoras]] the original source from which Greek philosophy developed. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4] "Neopythagorean" is a modern label that overlaps with "Middle Platonist" and "Neoplatonist", so one figure may be called a Neopythagorean by some scholars and a Platonist by others. Most of the figures below may be best regarded as part of the Platonic tradition, and one suggested description of them is "Pythagorising Platonists". [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.3] The earlier community around Pythagoras is covered in [Pythagoreans](platonism/Pythagoreans.md).

## Origins in the Academy

The Stanford Encyclopedia traces the probable origin of the movement to Plato's own school, the [[academy]], in the later fourth century BCE. Plato's successors Speusippus and Xenocrates both presented speculations that grew partly out of Plato's late metaphysics as the work of Pythagoras. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4] The Academy's side of this is told in [Academy](platonism/Academy.md).

Neopythagoreans typically stress number in the cosmos. They credit Pythagoras with the One and the Indefinite Dyad as ultimate principles, though these come from [[plato]]. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4] Interest in Pythagoreanism then declined for a couple of centuries, and the movement emerged again from the first century BCE. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4] The construct of Pythagoras that dominates this period has only limited contact with early Pythagoreanism, and there is little interest in an accurate account of the man. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4]

## Alexandria and Rome

The first Neopythagorean in the metaphysical sense was Eudorus of Alexandria, active in the middle and later first century BCE. Philo, a Jewish thinker of Alexandria one generation after Eudorus, used a Pythagorean theory of principles and Pythagorean number symbolism to give a philosophical reading of the Old Testament. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.3] Rouse Ball's history of mathematics, writing about the schools of [[alexandria]], says that the confusion of the change from republic to empire coincided with a shift in the prevailing views of philosophy, which were afterward mostly neo-platonic or neo-pythagorean. [@rouse-ball-short-account-history-mathematics, p. 78]

The Stanford Encyclopedia describes a strand that treats Pythagoras as a religious expert or a model of the moral life, tied to the prominence of Pythagoreanism in the literature of [[rome]] in the first century BCE and first century CE. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.5] It names Nigidius Figulus and Varro, the two most learned men in Rome in the first century BCE, as having connections to Pythagorean ritual practices. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.5]

The article sorts the movement into strands. One treats Pythagoras as a master metaphysician, a second stresses his place in the mathematical sciences, and a third stresses his practices. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.3] [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.4] [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.5] [[moderatus]] and [[numenius]] belong to the first, and [[nicomachus]] to the second.

## Moderatus

Moderatus of [[gades]], the modern Cadiz, was active in the first century CE. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.3] Plutarch labels him a Pythagorean and presents his follower Lucius as living by the Pythagorean taboos. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.3] His philosophy survives only in reports by other writers, so it is often hard to tell what is his and what belongs to the reporter. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.3]

He wrote an eleven-volume work, Lectures on Pythagoreanism, which Porphyry quotes in sections 48 to 53 of his Life of Pythagoras. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.3] There he argues that the Pythagoreans used numbers to teach clearly about bodiless forms and first principles, which words cannot express. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.3] In another excerpt he describes a system of three ones, apparently developed from the first two deductions in the second half of Plato's Parmenides. The first one is above being, the second is identified with the forms and accompanied by intelligible matter, and the third is identified with soul. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.3] The Stanford Encyclopedia says this system anticipates central ideas of [[plotinus]]. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.3]

Moderatus was a militant Neopythagorean. He charged that Plato, Aristotle and the early Academy took the fruitful parts of Pythagorean philosophy as their own with small changes and left the Pythagoreans only the superficial and trivial doctrines. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.3]

## Nicomachus

Nicomachus came from Gerasa, the modern Jerash in Jordan, and was probably active shortly before Numenius, in the earlier part of the second century CE. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.4] Two complete works of his survive, the Introduction to Arithmetic and the Handbook of Music, while the works of Eudorus, Moderatus and Numenius survive only in fragments. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.4] The Stanford Encyclopedia says that more than anyone else in antiquity he popularized supposed Pythagorean achievements in mathematics and the sciences. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.4]

The Handbook of Music tells the story of Pythagoras hearing the concords in the sound of hammers of different weights in a blacksmith's shop. The story is canonical but scientifically impossible, and [[iamblichus]] took it over almost word for word in the next century. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.4] The harmonic theory in the Handbook is not original and is somewhat retrograde. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.4]

The Introduction to Arithmetic had a long afterlife, though Nicomachus was not an original or particularly talented mathematician. Iamblichus, Asclepius of Tralles and Philoponus wrote commentaries on it, Apuleius translated it into Latin in the second half of the second century, and Boethius made what is virtually a translation of it, which became the standard work on arithmetic in the Middle Ages. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.4] In it Nicomachus divides reality into magnitude and multitude and says wisdom is knowledge of both. Four sciences study them, later called the quadrivium, which are arithmetic, music, geometry and astronomy. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.4] Arithmetic comes first because it existed in the mind of the creating god as the plan he followed in ordering the cosmos, so that numbers seem to replace the Platonic forms as the model of creation. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.4]

His Theology of Arithmetic survives only in reconstruction. It suggests that he largely returned to the principles of Plato's unwritten doctrines and did not copy Eudorus and Moderatus by placing a supreme god over the demiurge. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.4] He also wrote a Life of Pythagoras, now lost, which [[porphyry]] and Iamblichus used. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.4]

## Numenius

Numenius was a Platonist of the middle of the second century CE. [@sep-numenius, preamble] Very little is known of his life. The evidence ties him to two cities, [[apamea]] in Syria and Rome, and one way to reconcile it is that he was born in Apamea and taught there and later in Rome. [@sep-numenius, §1] The Stanford Encyclopedia on Pythagoreanism dates him to about 150 CE. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.3]

Ancient testimony divides over whether he was a Platonist or a Pythagorean, and the Stanford article on Numenius sees no contradiction in this, calling him a Pythagorean Platonist like Moderatus and Eudorus. He accepted both Pythagoras and Plato as authorities but held that Plato's authority depends on that of Pythagoras. [@sep-numenius, §2] He is reported to have asked "What else is Plato than Moses speaking Greek?" [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.3] He also made Socrates a Pythagorean. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.3]

His best attested work is On the Dissension of the Academics from Plato, a polemic aimed at the skeptical New Academy. [@sep-numenius, §1] [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.3] He wrote On the Good in at least six books, and On Numbers survives only as a title. [@sep-numenius, §1] Eusebius is the only ancient author who preserved quotations from him. [@sep-numenius, §5]

Numenius argued that Pythagoras treated the Dyad as a principle separate from the Monad, and he said that later thinkers who derived the Dyad from the Monad departed from the original teaching. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.3] He recognized three gods. The first is the good, simple and at rest. The second is the demiurge, who divides himself by organizing matter, and from this a third arises, identified with the ordered cosmos or the world soul. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.3]

## Plotinus and After

Numenius is where Neopythagoreanism has its clearest direct contact with Plotinus. Porphyry reports that Plotinus was accused of plagiarizing him, and that Amelius, a devotee of Numenius who followed Plotinus, wrote a treatise on the difference between the doctrines of the two. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.3] Numenius was one of the authors read in Plotinus's seminar. [@sep-numenius, preamble] The third-century Platonist Longinus went so far as to describe Plotinus as in a sense a Neopythagorean, naming Numenius, his follower Cronius, Moderatus and Thrasyllus as his predecessors. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.3]

The Stanford Encyclopedia does not accept the label for Plotinus himself. He was influenced by Neopythagorean speculation on first principles, but he treated Pythagoras as one predecessor among many and called Plato, not Pythagoras, divine. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.4] After Plotinus, Neopythagoreanism is absorbed into Neoplatonism. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.4] Iamblichus then carried out a systematic Pythagoreanization of Neoplatonism, particularly under the influence of Nicomachus on the quadrivium. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.4] [[plotinus]] has his own article.
