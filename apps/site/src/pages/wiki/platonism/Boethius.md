# Boethius

Boethius (born about 475 to 477, died probably 526) was a Roman who put Aristotle's logical works into Latin, commented on them, and wrote logical textbooks and the [[consolation-of-philosophy]] [@sep-boethius, preamble]. All of these writings drew heavily on Greek [[neoplatonists]], [[porphyry]] and [[iamblichus]] among them [@sep-boethius, preamble].

## Life

He was born into the Roman aristocracy about 475 to 477, near the time when Romulus Augustulus, the last Roman emperor, was deposed in 476 [@sep-boethius, §1]. Rouse Ball gives his birthplace as [[rome]] and his birth year as about 475 [@rouse-ball-short-account-history-mathematics, p. 110]. H. R. James says that his parents died early and that he was brought up by Symmachus, whose daughter he later married [@boethius-consolation-james, preamble].

He lived most of his life under Theoderic, an Ostrogoth who let the old families keep their traditions in Rome while he held power in Ravenna [@sep-boethius, §1]. He learned Greek thoroughly. The Stanford Encyclopedia thinks it unlikely that he went to Athens or Alexandria, where the last two Platonic schools were, but says he knew a good deal of the work done in them [@sep-boethius, §1]. Rouse Ball also calls the older belief that he was educated at Athens "somewhat doubtful" [@rouse-ball-short-account-history-mathematics, p. 110].

James says he was sole consul in 510 and that in 522 his two young sons were made joint consuls [@boethius-consolation-james, preamble]. Rouse Ball reports that Boethius seems to have wished for a life of study but entered politics because he accepted "that the world would be happy only when kings became philosophers or philosophers kings" [@rouse-ball-short-account-history-mathematics, p. 110].

He became Theoderic's Master of Offices, one of the most senior officials, and soon quarrelled with others at court, probably because he attacked their corruption [@sep-boethius, §1]. He was accused of treason and of magic and imprisoned, and he wrote the Consolation before he was executed, probably in 526 [@sep-boethius, §1]. James says that within a year of his sons' consulship he was a prisoner at [[pavia]] [@boethius-consolation-james, preamble], and a note to Book I names Pavia as the place of his imprisonment [@boethius-consolation-james, ch. IV]. Rouse Ball tells of his torture at Ticinum and his death by clubbing on 23 October 526, adding "Such at least is the account that has come down to us" [@rouse-ball-short-account-history-mathematics, p. 110].

## Logic and Porphyry

Besides textbooks on arithmetic and geometry closely based on Greek models, Boethius translated Aristotle's logic and commented on it, once on the Categories and twice each on On Interpretation and on Porphyry's Isagoge [@sep-boethius, §1]. Historians usually take this as the start of a larger plan, announced about 516 and ended by his execution, to translate and comment on all of Plato and Aristotle [@sep-boethius, §2].

The Stanford Encyclopedia stresses his choice of Porphyry as his main authority in logic [@sep-boethius, §2]. On the Categories he kept close to Porphyry's surviving question-and-answer commentary, and his long second commentary on On Interpretation is read as the best guide to Porphyry's lost one [@sep-boethius, §2]. A monograph by Porphyry on categorical syllogisms was the basis of Boethius's own work on that topic [@sep-porphyry, §3.4.1]. Later Greek Neoplatonists looked for Neoplatonic doctrine in Aristotle's logic, and because Boethius followed Porphyry, his commentaries were closer to Aristotle than the Greek ones of his day [@sep-boethius, §2].

Iamblichus's views on the quadrivium, which drew on Nicomachus, were taken up by Martianus Capella and Boethius and so entered medieval education [@sep-iamblichus, §4.2].

## Theological Treatises

In Treatises I, II and V of the Theological Treatises Boethius applied logic to problems of Christian doctrine, while Treatise IV is a plain statement of faith and Treatise III is a brief philosophical work that is not specifically Christian [@sep-boethius, §1]. The Stanford Encyclopedia reads the brief Treatise II as a faithful statement of [[augustine]]'s view that what is said of God is either relative, as with "Father" and "Son", or substantial, and Treatise I as Boethius's development of it [@sep-boethius, §4].

## The Consolation of Philosophy

The Consolation is a prosimetrum, prose with verse between, in which the prisoner Boethius talks with a woman who personifies Philosophy [@sep-boethius, §1]. It makes no explicit reference to Christianity, which the Stanford Encyclopedia counts a special problem in the last work of a Christian facing death [@sep-boethius, §5].

[[plato]] is named in it. In Book I Boethius tells Philosophy that she taught him through Plato "that states would be happy, either if philosophers ruled them, or if it should so befall that their rulers would turn philosophers" [@boethius-consolation-james, ch. IV]. James's list of references traces the maxim to the [[republic]] [@boethius-consolation-james, bk. V]. In Book III Philosophy cites the [[timaeus]] for the rule that "we ought even in the most trivial matters to implore the Divine protection" [@boethius-consolation-james, ch. IX]. The poem that follows, III m. 9, is the most solemn in the work and invokes God in terms borrowed from the Timaeus [@sep-boethius, §5]. James's note says its substance comes from Timaeus 29 to 42 [@boethius-consolation-james, bk. III].

From III.10 Philosophy argues from the Neoplatonic assumptions Boethius accepts that the perfect good and perfect happiness are God himself [@sep-boethius, §5]. In Book IV she draws on Plato's Gorgias to show that the wicked do not really prosper and have no power [@sep-boethius, §5].

## Foreknowledge, Eternity and Proclus

In Book V the character Boethius argues that if God foreknows every act of will, there can be no freedom of choice [@sep-boethius, §6]. Philosophy answers that a thing is known according to the capacity of the knower and not its own power [@sep-boethius, §6]. God is eternal, and she defines eternity as "the possession of endless life whole and perfect at a single moment" [@boethius-consolation-james, ch. VI]. Such a being knows past, present and future events as we know what is present, so events future to us can be known by God as certain [@sep-boethius, §6].

The Stanford Encyclopedia's article on [[proclus]] says that Proclus answered the same problem with the principle that the mode of knowledge depends on the knower, an answer that goes back to Iamblichus, and that the Consolation took it up in Book V, chapter 6 [@sep-proclus, §3.4]. Whether Boethius also shared Proclus's determinism is disputed. He is usually read as an indeterminist, but Noble (2024), as the Stanford Encyclopedia reports, argues that he adopted Proclus's position, in which God finally determines everything while an event stays contingent until causes in time fix it [@sep-boethius, §6].

## Influence

His translations of Aristotle and Porphyry stayed standard throughout the Middle Ages, except for the Posterior Analytics, which was lost [@sep-boethius, §8]. The Stanford Encyclopedia lists Plato, [[aristotle]], Alexander of Aphrodisias, Porphyry and Iamblichus as the sources of the ideas and arguments he made available [@sep-boethius, §8]. Rouse Ball says that early medieval teaching of mathematics rested mainly on his geometry and arithmetic [@rouse-ball-short-account-history-mathematics, p. 110].

The Consolation was translated into almost every medieval vernacular and into Greek and Hebrew [@sep-boethius, §8]. Jean de Meun put it into Old French in the later thirteenth century, and Chaucer into Middle English about a century later [@sep-boethius, §8]. Rouse Ball notes Alfred the Great's translation into Anglo-Saxon [@rouse-ball-short-account-history-mathematics, p. 110].
