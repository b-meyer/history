# Augustine

Augustine of Hippo (354 to 430) was a Christian theologian and bishop. [@eor-augustine-hippo, preamble] The Stanford Encyclopedia calls Platonism a decisive ingredient of his thought and reads him as a Christian philosopher of late antiquity who was in constant dialogue with the classical tradition throughout his work. [@sep-augustine, preamble] Through him Neoplatonism shaped Christian theology in the Latin West. [@sep-neoplatonism, §8]

## Life

Augustine was born on 13 November 354 at [[thagaste]] in Roman Africa, modern Souk Ahras in Algeria. [@sep-augustine, §1] His father Patricius was a pagan and his mother Monica a Christian. [@eor-augustine-hippo, preamble] He studied grammar and rhetoric at Madauros and at [[carthage]], and at about eighteen he took a mistress in Carthage, who bore him a son, Adeodatus. [@sep-augustine, §1] From about 373 he was a "hearer" of Manicheism, a dualist religion, and stayed with it for nine years. [@sep-augustine, §1] In the Confessions he tells how grief at a friend's death drove him from Thagaste back to Carthage. [@augustine-confessions-pusey, bk. IV]

Unruly students at Carthage led him to leave for [[rome]], where he fell ill and then taught rhetoric. [@eor-augustine-hippo, preamble] The Confessions present the move as a plan to teach at Rome what he had been teaching at Carthage. [@augustine-confessions-pusey, bk. V] In 383 he moved to [[milan]], then the western capital, as the city's professor of rhetoric. [@sep-augustine, §1] There Bishop Ambrose taught him to read Scripture allegorically, and Christians with Neoplatonic leanings showed him a philosophically informed Christianity. [@sep-augustine, §1]

A period of doubt ended in the summer of 386, when he converted to ascetic Christianity and gave up his chair of rhetoric. [@sep-augustine, §1] In his own account he heard a child's voice chanting "Take up and read," opened the letters of the Apostle, and read the first passage his eyes fell on. [@augustine-confessions-pusey, bk. VIII] Ambrose baptized him at Milan at Easter 387, together with his son and his friend Alypius. [@eor-augustine-hippo, preamble] On the way back to Africa his mother died at Ostia, which the Stanford Encyclopedia dates to 388. [@sep-augustine, §1] The Encyclopedia of Religion has him return to Rome after her death and write there before sailing for Africa. [@eor-augustine-hippo, preamble] The Stanford Encyclopedia takes him from Ostia back to Africa with no stay at Rome. [@sep-augustine, §1]

Back in Africa he set up a lay monastery on his small estate at Thagaste. [@eor-augustine-hippo, preamble] In 391 he was ordained priest at [[hippo]] Regius, apparently against his will, and about 396 he succeeded the local bishop. [@sep-augustine, §1] As a priest he took [[paul]] as his guide and found in him his theological mentor. [@eor-augustine-hippo, preamble] His mature doctrine of grace seems to have grown out of a new reading of Paul around 395. [@sep-augustine, §4] He died at Hippo on 28 August 430, while the Vandals besieged the city. [@eor-augustine-hippo, preamble] His biographer Possidius reports that he died with a word of [[plotinus]] on his lips. [@sep-augustine, §1]

## The Books of the Platonists

At Milan, Augustine and his circle came to [[plato]] through the teaching of Plotinus. [@eor-augustine-hippo, preamble] In the Confessions he says he was given "certain books of the Platonists, translated from Greek into Latin". [@augustine-confessions-pusey, bk. VII] He names the translator as Victorinus, a former professor of rhetoric at Rome who, as Augustine had heard, had died a Christian. [@augustine-confessions-pusey, bk. VIII] He read them in 386 but never says which authors or treatises they contained. [@sep-augustine, §4]

Which works they were is debated. Twentieth-century scholars argued over whether Plotinus or [[porphyry]] was the main Neoplatonic influence on him, and most now accept that the books held some treatises of Plotinus and a selection from Porphyry. [@sep-augustine, §4] The Stanford Encyclopedia entry on Porphyry also thinks they probably included works by both. [@sep-porphyry, §2] Augustine went on reading Neoplatonists after 386, and around 400 he had Porphyry's Philosophy from the Oracles to hand. [@sep-augustine, §4] The entry on Neoplatonism describes him as intimately familiar with the writings of Plotinus and Porphyry. [@sep-neoplatonism, §1]

Augustine says that these books taught him to look for incorporeal truth. [@augustine-confessions-pusey, bk. VII] He says he found in them the substance of the opening of John's Gospel, but not that the Word came among his own and was not received by them. [@augustine-confessions-pusey, bk. VII] In Plato he found answers on the origin of evil, the question that had first drawn him to the Manicheans. [@eor-augustine-hippo, preamble]

The Stanford Encyclopedia calls Neoplatonism the most lasting philosophical influence on Augustine. [@sep-augustine, §4] Among the features of his thought it traces to Neoplatonic sources are a transcendent and immaterial God, the ranking of God over soul and soul over body, Forms in the mind of God, and evil as a privation of good. [@sep-augustine, §4] A further Platonic element is the ascent of the mind from bodies to the soul and from the sensible to the intelligible, which ends in God. [@sep-augustine, §4]

## The Confessions and the City of God

The [[confessions]], written about 396 to 400, is probably his most original work. [@sep-augustine, §2] He began it two years after becoming bishop, at the age of forty-three. [@eor-augustine-hippo, preamble]

The sack of Rome by Alaric in 410 prompted the [[city-of-god]]. [@sep-augustine, §1] Augustine began it in 412 and finished it in 426. [@sep-augustine, §2] The Encyclopedia of Religion dates the work to 413 to 427. [@eor-augustine-hippo, preamble]

Book VIII ranks the Platonists above all other philosophers, and Augustine writes that "none come nearer to us than the Platonists." [@augustine-city-of-god-dods, CG 8.5] There he grants that Platonism and Christianity agree that God is the first principle, the highest good and the standard of knowledge. [@sep-augustine, §4]

Book X turns the comparison against the Platonists. A chapter heading speaks of the incarnation, "which the Platonists in their impiety blush to acknowledge." [@augustine-city-of-god-dods, CG 10.29] Augustine tells Porphyry that he sees from far off the country where we should live but does not know the way to it. [@augustine-city-of-god-dods, CG 10.29] Porphyry had admitted that he had found no universal way for the soul's deliverance, and Augustine concludes that the philosophy he followed was either not the truest or did not contain that way. [@augustine-city-of-god-dods, CG 10.32] On the Stanford Encyclopedia's reading, Platonism in the City of God cannot bring salvation because it is unable or unwilling to accept Christ as mediator, and so it falls short as philosophy too. [@sep-augustine, §4]

The dispute with Porphyry was older than the City of God. After 400 Augustine answered objections to Christianity that seem to have been taken from Porphyry's Against the Christians. [@sep-augustine, §1] Madec described Augustine as at once "disciple and adversary of Porphyry". [@sep-porphyry, §2]

## Legacy

Porphyry's mark on the Latin West came above all through Augustine. [@sep-porphyry, §2] The Stanford Encyclopedia names Augustine in the West, beside the Cappadocian Fathers in the East, as a channel through which Neoplatonism reached Christian theology. [@sep-neoplatonism, §8]

The Stanford Encyclopedia ties [[boethius]] to Augustine through the theological treatises. Augustine distinguished relative from substantial predication about God, Boethius set out this view faithfully in his second treatise, and the treatises use logical analysis in theology, a method Augustine had anticipated without developing it. [@sep-boethius, §4]
