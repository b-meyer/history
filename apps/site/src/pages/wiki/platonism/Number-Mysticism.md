# Number Mysticism from Pythagoras to Proclus

Aristotle reported that the Pythagoreans thought things were numbers, or were made of numbers. [@sep-philolaus, §3] This page follows that report and the doctrines that grew from it through the people covered elsewhere in this collection.

## Philolaus and the Early Pythagoreans

[[philolaus]] wrote that the cosmos and everything in it was fitted together out of limiting and unlimited things. [@sep-philolaus, preamble] The fitting together, which he called a "harmonia," can be described mathematically, and his main example is a musical scale in which the octave, the fifth and the fourth have the ratios 2 to 1, 3 to 2 and 4 to 3. [@sep-philolaus, preamble]

In Fragment 4 he says that all things that are known have number. The Stanford article reads this as a claim about knowledge, since number is what allows things to be known and understood. [@sep-philolaus, §3] Fragment 5 says that number has two proper kinds, odd and even, and a third mixed from both, the even-odd. [@sep-philolaus, §3]

The same article doubts that Philolaus said things are numbers. In the surviving fragments he describes the components of things as limiters and unlimiteds and never says they arise from numbers. The article takes Aristotle's report to be Aristotle's own reading of the system, and it names Schibli and Schofield as defenders of the report. [@sep-philolaus, §3] Burkert took Philolaus's appeal to number to be an instance of Pythagorean number mysticism with little to do with the rigorous mathematics of his contemporaries. The Stanford article replies that the mathematics of the authentic Fragment 6a is above reproach. [@sep-philolaus, §3]

Aristotle also described other Pythagoreans who set out ten pairs of opposites, among them limit and unlimited, odd and even, and unity and plurality. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §2.4] Their table put good in one column and bad in the other, and nothing in the fragments of Philolaus says that limit is good and unlimited bad. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §2.4]

For [[pythagoras]] himself the early evidence gives no sign that his fame rested primarily on mathematics or cosmology. [@sep-pythagoras, §5] The Stanford article on Philolaus still finds number in the acousmata, the sayings that probably go back to Pythagoras. One says that number is the wisest thing, and Pythagoreans swore oaths by "the one who gave the tetraktys," a name for the first four numbers, whose sum of ten was the perfect number. The evidence for the oath is late. [@sep-philolaus, §7.1] The article concludes that Pythagoras seems to have presented a cosmos ordered by symbolic numbers, and that Philolaus continued the tradition by placing ten celestial bodies around the central fire because ten is the perfect number. [@sep-philolaus, §7.1] The [[pythagoreans]] page covers the school itself.

## The Tetractys in Iamblichus

Iamblichus says Pythagoras often told his disciples that all things accord in number, and that the companions who lived together swore by "him who the tetractys found." [@iamblichus-pythagoric-life-taylor, ch. XXIX] Both the maxim and the oath come from a late biography, and the Stanford article on Pythagoreanism treats that biography as largely a compilation of earlier sources. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.4]

Thomas Taylor's notes to the translation say that the sum of 1, 2, 3 and 4 is ten, and that the tetractys holds the harmonic ratios of the octave, fifth and fourth. [@iamblichus-pythagoric-life-taylor, preamble] Quoting Theo of Smyrna, Taylor adds that the Pythagoreans honored the tetractys because all symphonies are found in it and because it appears to contain the nature of all things. He lists eleven tetractys, which include the simple bodies, the seasons of the year and the ages of man. [@iamblichus-pythagoric-life-taylor, preamble] Another note, which Taylor says draws chiefly on [[syrianus]], says the Pythagoreans called forms numbers. It refers the point to the monad, the line to the duad, the surface to the triad and the solid to the tetrad. [@iamblichus-pythagoric-life-taylor, preamble]

## Plato and the Academy

The Stanford article on Philolaus finds that [[plato]] had studied Philolaus's system by the time of the Philebus. It points to the limit and unlimited as basic principles, to the claim that number is the basis of all inquiring, learning and teaching, and to the musical scale as the central example. [@sep-philolaus, §7.2] It adds that Plato was not taking the system over whole. He was using it to address problems that had arisen with the theory of forms. [@sep-philolaus, §7.2] Aristotle says Plato differed from the Pythagoreans in making the unlimited a dyad. [@sep-philolaus, §7.2]

In the [[republic]] [[socrates]] says the future rulers should study arithmetic until they see the nature of numbers with the mind only, because it is the easiest way for the soul to pass from becoming to being. [@plato-republic-jowett, bk. VII] He says the masters of the art rebuff anyone who tries to divide absolute unity, and that the numbers they speak of can be realized only in thought. [@plato-republic-jowett, bk. VII] Harmony and astronomy are called sister sciences, "as the Pythagoreans say." [@plato-republic-jowett, bk. VII] The same article on Philolaus notes that Plato criticizes the Pythagoreans at R. 531c for looking for numbers in things instead of numbers themselves. [@sep-philolaus, §3]

Diogenes Laertius gives a Pythagorean account in which the monad is the principle of all things. Numbers come from the monad together with the undefined dyad. Numbers give rise to points, points to lines, and lines to plane figures, then solids and sensible bodies. [@diogenes-laertius-lives-hicks, DL 8.25] The Stanford article on Pythagoras says this is the system most commonly ascribed to him in the later tradition, but that Aristotle makes clear it was Plato's system in his later years. Aristotle says the indefinite dyad is unique to Plato. [@sep-pythagoras, §2.2] The most convincing explanation the article offers is that Plato's successors in the [[academy]], [[speusippus]], [[xenocrates]] and Heraclides, presented Pythagoreanism as having anticipated late Platonic metaphysics. The article adds that some scholars reject this thesis. [@sep-pythagoras, §2.2]

Speusippus wrote a book On Pythagorean Numbers that seems to build on early ideas such as ten as the perfect number, although the source cannot be sure that the title goes back to him or that he assigned all its ideas to the Pythagoreans, and Zhmud regards the perfection of ten as Platonic rather than Pythagorean. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.1] Xenocrates defined the soul as "a number moving itself," a definition the doxographers gave to Pythagoras and that Burkert thinks Xenocrates may have developed from the Timaeus. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.1]

## The Neopythagoreans

The [[neopythagoreans]] page covers the people in detail. On number, the Stanford article reports that Eudorus had the Pythagoreans posit one supreme principle, with the monad and the indefinite dyad below it as elements, not principles. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.3] Moderatus argued that the Pythagoreans used numbers to teach clearly about bodiless forms and first principles that cannot be put in words. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.3] [[numenius]] held that Pythagoras treated the dyad as a principle of its own, apart from the monad, and he is said to have written a work on numbers to which he gave an ontological role. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.3] [@sep-numenius, §2]

[[nicomachus]] left the textbook that carried the doctrine to later readers. In his Introduction to Arithmetic he presents arithmetic as the most important of the four sciences, because it existed in the mind of the creating god as the plan he followed in ordering the cosmos, so that numbers appear to replace the Platonic forms as the model of creation. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.4] His Theology of Arithmetic may have been most influential in setting up an equivalence between the pagan gods and the numbers of the decad, which Iamblichus and Proclus later took up. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.4]

## Plotinus and Porphyry

The Stanford article does not count [[plotinus]] as a Neopythagorean. He was influenced by Neopythagorean speculation on first principles, but he treated Pythagoras as one predecessor among many and called Plato, not Pythagoras, divine. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.4] Number has its own treatise in the [[enneads]], VI.6 "On Numbers," which begins from the suggestion that multiplicity is a falling away from the Unity and that limitlessness is the complete departure. [@plotinus-enneads-mackenna, Enn. 6.6.1]

He asks whether the numbers in the Intellectual are Ideas added to the other Ideas or only necessary concomitants of them. [@plotinus-enneads-mackenna, Enn. 6.6.4] He considers numbers as the Pythagoreans seem to hold them in their symbolic system, where Justice is a Tetrad, and says this adds a number to a thing that is already one thing. [@plotinus-enneads-mackenna, Enn. 6.6.5] His own answer is that Number comes before the beings. Being is Number collective, the beings are Number unfolded, Intellectual-Principle is Number moving within itself, and the Living-Form is Number container of the universe. [@plotinus-enneads-mackenna, Enn. 6.6.9] The Forms have been called Henads and Numbers, and the authentic Number is the one inherent in Authentic Being, of which the monadic number is an image. [@plotinus-enneads-mackenna, Enn. 6.6.9] Being rests on the Unity, and the Unity does not rest on Being. [@plotinus-enneads-mackenna, Enn. 6.6.9] He argues that the total of the beings is not a matter of chance, so Number is a causing principle that comes before it. [@plotinus-enneads-mackenna, Enn. 6.6.10] Stanford's article on Plotinus adds that the arrangement of the Forms within Intellect is determined by eidetic numbers. [@sep-plotinus, §6]

[[porphyry]] put number into the shape of the book. He divided the fifty-four treatises into six sets of nine, and he says he liked pairing the perfect number six with nines. [@porphyry-life-of-plotinus-mackenna, p. 24] The Stanford article calls the result an arithmetical schema and says it has been criticized as artificial and arbitrary, closer to the editor's views than to those of Plotinus. [@sep-plotinus, §1] Porphyry's Life of Pythagoras, by the same article's account, assigns the Platonic distinction between intelligible and sensible realms back to Pythagoras. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.4]

## Iamblichus

The Stanford article credits [[iamblichus]] with a systematic Pythagoreanization of Neoplatonism, under the influence of Nicomachus. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.4] His On Pythagoreanism ran to ten books. Book III treated mathematical science in general, Book IV was a commentary on Nicomachus's Introduction to Arithmetic, and Books V to VII dealt with arithmetic in physics, ethics and theology. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.4] For the last of these he appears to have relied again on Nicomachus's Theology of Arithmetic. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.4]

He placed the Pythagorean pair of Limit and the Unlimited below the One and above the Intelligible. [@sep-iamblichus, §4.1] The Stanford article says the pair reached Neoplatonism chiefly through Plato's Philebus and Aristotle's report on the Pythagoreans. [@sep-iamblichus, §4.1] It also says that Damascius linked the intelligible triad of Limit, the Unlimited and One-Being to a Pythagorean sequence of Monad, Dyad and Triad, and that Iamblichus likely made the same link. [@sep-iamblichus, §3.1]

Mathematics, for Iamblichus, is both a science and a realm of being between the intelligible and the sensible, with the Pythagorean proviso that each number has its own essence. [@sep-iamblichus, §4.2] Studying it lets the soul disengage from the material world. [@sep-iamblichus, §4.2] He wrote that to practice mathematics in a Pythagorean manner one must follow its "divinely inspired, elevating, purifying and perfective way." [@sep-iamblichus, §4.3] Syrianus's distinction among eidetic, mathematical and physical number very likely stems from his teaching. [@sep-iamblichus, §4.2]

## Proclus

[[proclus]] is reported to have dreamed that he was the reincarnated soul of the Pythagorean Nicomachus. [@marinus-life-of-proclus-guthrie, Marinus 28] The Stanford article on Pythagoreanism says that it was because of Iamblichus that numerology and mathematics were emphasized by Syrianus and Proclus. It adds that Proclus found Plato clearer than the Pythagoreans, though his Platonism is still heavily Pythagorean. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.4]

In his [[elements-of-theology]], Proposition 1 says all multitude participates in a certain respect in the one. [@proclus-elements-of-theology-taylor, ET 1] Every divine number is unical, Proclus argues, because its cause is the one and the one is God. [@proclus-elements-of-theology-taylor, ET 113] He also identifies gods with self-perfect unities, in both directions. [@proclus-elements-of-theology-taylor, ET 114] The multitude of the divine unities is bounded according to number, because it lies nearest the one and the infinite is foreign to the one. [@proclus-elements-of-theology-taylor, ET 149] The multitude of intellects is bounded for the same reason. [@proclus-elements-of-theology-taylor, ET 179]

In his commentary on Euclid, Proclus holds that mathematical objects cannot be abstracted from sensible particulars. They reside primarily in intellect and secondarily in souls, and geometry leads the soul upward to the unextended causes of geometrical objects in the divine mind. [@sep-proclus, §3.5] Iamblichus's writings on mathematics were used in that commentary. [@sep-iamblichus, §4.2] Boethius later made what is virtually a translation of Nicomachus's Introduction to Arithmetic, which became the standard work on arithmetic in the Middle Ages. [@sep-pythagoreanism, §4.4]
