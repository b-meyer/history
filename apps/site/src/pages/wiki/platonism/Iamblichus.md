# Iamblichus

Iamblichus (about 242 to about 325) was a Syrian Neoplatonist and a disciple of [[porphyry]]. [@sep-iamblichus, preamble] The Stanford Encyclopedia counts him as one of the three major representatives of early Neoplatonism, with Plotinus as another. [@sep-iamblichus, preamble] He argued for salvation by ritual means, known as theurgy, and the Stanford entry ties his sharp criticism of Plotinus to his bleak view of the human soul's state. [@sep-iamblichus, preamble]

## Life

Little is known about his life, and the Stanford Encyclopedia calls Eunapius's biography of him merely hagiographical. [@sep-iamblichus, §1.1] Tradition places his birth at Chalcis ad Belum, modern Qinnasrin, though some have argued for Chalcis sub Libanum. [@sep-iamblichus, §1.1] Eunapius says only that he was born at [[chalcis]], a city in Coele Syria. [@eunapius-lives-wright, p. 363] He came from a Syrian family of high standing, the Sampsigeramids of Emesa, who had reached the imperial throne with Elagabalus. [@sep-iamblichus, §1.1]

Eunapius makes him first a pupil of Anatolius and then a follower of Porphyry. [@eunapius-lives-wright, p. 363] The Stanford Encyclopedia finds no decisive reason to doubt this. [@sep-iamblichus, §1.1] Porphyry dedicated a treatise to Iamblichus, and since Porphyry names Iamblichus's son as married to a Roman aristocrat, the entry takes it as very likely that Iamblichus studied under Porphyry at Rome. [@sep-iamblichus, §1.1] The entry on Porphyry says it is clear that Iamblichus was strongly influenced by Porphyry, even though he later reacted vehemently against some of his positions. [@sep-porphyry, §1] Eunapius rates him as in no respect inferior to Porphyry except in style, which he finds neither charming nor clear. [@eunapius-lives-wright, p. 363] For Porphyry himself, see [Porphyry](platonism/Porphyry.md).

At some point after he parted from Porphyry, a break the Stanford entry says cannot be dated precisely, Iamblichus settled in Syria. [@sep-iamblichus, §1.1] He owned an estate at Daphne, a suburb of Antioch, but the Stanford Encyclopedia accepts that he settled at [[apamea]], where Plotinus's first disciple Amelius had already moved. [@sep-iamblichus, §1.1] There a wealthy notable named Sopater welcomed him, and he founded a school of which about a dozen students are known. [@sep-iamblichus, §1.1] Eunapius says that students came to him from all parts. [@eunapius-lives-wright, p. 363] He names Sopater the Syrian, Aedesius and Eustathius from Cappadocia, and Theodorus and Euphrasius from Greece. [@eunapius-lives-wright, p. 365] The Stanford Encyclopedia warns that Eunapius is a tendentious source with a hagiographical agenda. [@sep-iamblichus, §2.4]

Eunapius relays a rumor, said to have reached the students through his slaves, that when Iamblichus prayed he rose to all appearance more than ten cubits from the ground. [@eunapius-lives-wright, p. 365] He reports that Iamblichus laughed and said the story came from a witty fellow who had deceived them. [@eunapius-lives-wright, p. 367] Iamblichus wrote a biography of Alypius after the latter died. [@eunapius-lives-wright, p. 375] Eunapius says that Alypius, an Alexandrian by birth, died an old man at Alexandria and that Iamblichus died after him. [@eunapius-lives-wright, p. 377] He adds that Iamblichus's pupils then scattered in different directions. [@eunapius-lives-wright, p. 379]

## Works

Few of his works survive in the direct manuscript tradition. [@sep-iamblichus, §1.2] Four of them belong to a larger work on Pythagoreanism. These are [[on-the-pythagorean-way-of-life]], the Protrepticus, a treatise on general mathematical science, and an introduction to arithmetic that follows the treatise of [[nicomachus]]. [@sep-iamblichus, §1.2] His most important surviving treatise answers Porphyry's Letter to Anebo, under the title Reply to Porphyry or, since Ficino, De mysteriis. [@sep-iamblichus, §1.2] It defends sacrifice, prayer and divination against Porphyry's objections, and Iamblichus wrote it under the guise of an Egyptian prophet, Abamon. [@sep-iamblichus, §1.2] His commentaries on Plato and Aristotle are all lost. For two of them, the Categories commentary known through Simplicius and the Timaeus commentary known through Proclus, the quotations are numerous enough to establish the main features of his exegesis. [@sep-iamblichus, §1.2]

## Place in Neoplatonism

Karl Praechter called Iamblichus the second founder of Neoplatonism after Plotinus. [@sep-iamblichus, §2.1] The Stanford Encyclopedia qualifies this. "Neoplatonism" is a modern category, and it finds no institutional continuity between the school of Plotinus and that of Iamblichus. [@sep-iamblichus, §2.1] Iamblichus accepted and developed features of Plotinus's philosophy, including his account of the One and the first principles, and can be seen as a follower of Plotinus and Porphyry. [@sep-iamblichus, §2.1] He brought about a shift toward religion and theology in Neoplatonism, and he is the first Neoplatonist to develop a "Platonic theology". [@sep-iamblichus, §2.3]

His views on embodied souls, on theurgy, on a harmonizing account of the theological traditions with a special role for Pythagoras and the Chaldean Oracles, and on a hierarchy with more intermediate levels separate him from Plotinus and Porphyry and prepare the later school of Proclus. [@sep-iamblichus, §2.4] The Stanford Encyclopedia on [[proclus]] describes his work as continuing a movement that Iamblichus inaugurated. [@sep-proclus, preamble] More than a century separates Iamblichus's death from the founding of the school at Athens by Plutarch of Athens, and how his teaching reached Athens is unknown. [@sep-iamblichus, §2.4] In the fourth century Themistius did not endorse his philosophy and criticized the "man of Chalcis". [@sep-iamblichus, §2.4]

## The System

The Stanford Encyclopedia describes his system as a refined version of Plotinus's, which builds on hints in the Enneads and on Porphyry's work and gives a far more complex picture of reality. [@sep-iamblichus, §3] He reacted against Porphyry's first principle coordinated with the Intelligible Triad by placing two levels above it, a wholly unspeakable principle and a principle uncoordinated with the triad. [@sep-iamblichus, §3.1] Below the One he placed the intelligible triad of Limit, the Unlimited and the One-Being. [@sep-iamblichus, §3.1]

His best-known doctrine may be the distinction between the intelligible (noēton) and the intellective (noeron). [@sep-iamblichus, §3.2] From Iamblichus onward noeron is a technical term for the order of gods right below the Intelligible, which has demiurgic functions. [@sep-iamblichus, §3.2] Where Plotinus had argued that intelligibles are not outside the Intellect, Iamblichus subordinated the Demiurge to the Intelligible Model. [@sep-iamblichus, §3.2]

## Mathematics and Pythagoreanism

Iamblichus placed the Pythagorean principles of Limit and the Unlimited high in his system, below the One and above the Intelligible. [@sep-iamblichus, §4.1] The same pair works among mathematical entities, in a role like that of Monad and Dyad or Odd and Even for Nicomachus of Gerasa, the Neopythagorean author of the Introduction to Arithmetic and one of Iamblichus's intellectual heroes. [@sep-iamblichus, §4.2] The Stanford Encyclopedia lists four mathematical sciences, arithmetic, geometry, music and astronomy, and says that arithmetic takes precedence. [@sep-iamblichus, §4.2]

He took mathematics to be both a science and a realm of being between the intelligible and the sensible, with the Pythagorean proviso that each number has its own essence. [@sep-iamblichus, §4.2] Studying it lets the soul disengage from the material world. [@sep-iamblichus, §4.2] Syrianus's later distinction among eidetic, mathematical and physical number very likely stems from his teaching. [@sep-iamblichus, §4.2] His views on the quadrivium, drawn from Nicomachus, were adopted by Martianus Capella and Boethius, and through them entered medieval education. [@sep-iamblichus, §4.2] The thread from Pythagoras to Proclus is followed in [Number Mysticism from Pythagoras to Proclus](platonism/Number-Mysticism.md).

On his own account, the true meaning of mathematics is not technical but a form of philosophical ascesis. [@sep-iamblichus, §4.3] As part of his program of agreement among authorities, he made Pythagoras the recipient of a divine revelation handed down by Orpheus. [@sep-iamblichus, §4.3]

## Soul and Theurgy

In the Reply to Porphyry Iamblichus distinguishes three disciplines, philosophy, theology and theurgy. [@sep-iamblichus, §2.3] Theurgy goes beyond reason and philosophy, but the Stanford Encyclopedia says his appeal to it rests on philosophical arguments about divine beings and human souls. [@sep-iamblichus, §2.3] Scholars once called his stance irrational, and recent scholarship has questioned that view. [@sep-iamblichus, §2.3]

He held that the human soul cannot achieve lasting union with the intelligible world and needs help from the gods. [@sep-iamblichus, §6.3] Its purpose is co-action with the gods, theourgia, reached through prayer, offerings and sacrifice, the liturgy that Porphyry had attacked in the Letter to Anebo. [@sep-iamblichus, §6.3] Ritual takes precedence over philosophical ascesis, though Iamblichus would say he is giving philosophy its due place. [@sep-iamblichus, §6.3] The Stanford Encyclopedia calls this stance anti-intellectualist and in stark contrast with the teachings of Plotinus and Porphyry. [@sep-iamblichus, §6.3] Julian the Apostate was among the later proponents of a pagan revival who embraced his views. [@sep-iamblichus, §6.3]

## The Pythagorean Life

The Stanford Encyclopedia calls [[on-the-pythagorean-way-of-life]] one of the major sources on ancient Pythagoreanism. [@sep-iamblichus, §4.3] It also says that the work plagiarizes earlier biographies of Pythagoras, and that it shows Iamblichus's conception of the divine man. [@sep-iamblichus, §1.2] A reader has to weigh how far Iamblichus depended on his sources and how far he reworked them for his own purposes. [@sep-iamblichus, §4.3] The work is therefore evidence both for Pythagoras and for Iamblichus's own tradition.

In the English translation by Thomas Taylor, the work tells that Pythagoras on his return to Samos taught arithmetic and geometry to a young athlete, paying him three obols for each figure he learned. [@iamblichus-pythagoric-life-taylor, ch. V] The same chapter says that Pythagoras then went to Italy, where at Crotona he gained about six hundred followers. [@iamblichus-pythagoric-life-taylor, ch. V] Later in the work, Cylon of Croton is said to have been refused admission to the Pythagorean life, after which he and his friends acted violently against Pythagoras and his disciples. [@iamblichus-pythagoric-life-taylor, ch. XXXV] For the life of Pythagoras itself, see [Pythagoras](platonism/Pythagoras.md).
