# Ibn Rushd

Ibn Rushd (1126 to 1198) was an Andalusian philosopher, physician and judge. [@sep-ibn-rushd, preamble] He held that philosophy can reach demonstrative certainty in many fields and that [[aristotle]] should be its chief guide. [@sep-ibn-rushd, preamble] Latin writers called him Averroes, a corrupted form of his name, and knew him simply as "the Commentator." [@sep-ibn-rushd, preamble]

## Life

He was born at [[cordoba]] in 1126 into an Andalusian family known for its judges and its learning in the religious sciences. [@sep-ibn-rushd, §1.1] His grandfather, also named Ibn Rushd, was a judge and a jurist of the Maliki school, so the philosopher is called "the grandson" to tell the two apart. [@sep-ibn-rushd, §1.1] He left no autobiography, and many parts of his life remain obscure. [@sep-ibn-rushd, §1.1]

The Almohads came to power in the western Maghreb and Andalusia when he was 22. [@sep-ibn-rushd, §1.1] He moved in the ruling circles of [[marrakesh]], and the second Almohad ruler, Abu Yaqub Yusuf (reigned 1163 to 1184), asked him to comment on Aristotle's works and make them accessible. [@sep-ibn-rushd, §1.1] He later became chief physician to the caliph in Marrakesh. [@sep-ibn-rushd, §1.1] The Encyclopedia of Religion says that he lived in Marrakesh for many years and wrote some of his most important works there. [@eor-islam-north-africa, preamble]

Late in his career he fell out of favour with the Almohads, for reasons that remain unclear, and was exiled to Lucena, southeast of Cordoba. [@sep-ibn-rushd, §1.1] The Encyclopedia of Religion gives 1198 as the year of his death. [@eor-islam-north-africa, preamble] The Stanford Encyclopedia reports one account in which he died that year while confined to a residence in Marrakesh. [@sep-ibn-rushd, §1.1]

## Commentaries on Aristotle

His writings cover medicine, logic and every branch of philosophy, along with Islamic law, theology and Arabic grammar. [@sep-ibn-rushd, §1.2] Many are commentaries, above all on Aristotle, covering nearly the whole Aristotelian corpus. [@sep-ibn-rushd, §1.2] He wrote of Aristotle that no one in the close to 1500 years since had been able to "add a word worthy of attention to what he said." [@sep-ibn-rushd, §1.2] The Stanford Encyclopedia on the Greek sources of Arabic philosophy traces how the philosophers of the Muslim West prepared his plan to comment on the Aristotelian corpus "as the sum of philosophical science." [@sep-arabic-islamic-greek, §5]

Scholars have sorted the commentaries into three kinds, the short commentary or epitome, the middle commentary or paraphrase, and the long commentary, though recent work doubts that the threefold scheme fits his practice. [@sep-ibn-rushd, §1.2]

## Al-Farabi and Ibn Sina

His long commentary on the Posterior Analytics is the occasion for "a critical and severe interaction" with [[al-farabi]] and [[ibn-sina]], the two Islamic authorities in logic before him. [@sep-ibn-rushd, §2] Ibn Sina's views are often the target of his logic, and he rejects Ibn Sina's denial that a demonstration of existence counts as a demonstration. [@sep-ibn-rushd, §2] On God he sets his own account against Ibn Sina's "insufficiently Aristotelian philosophy" and rejects Ibn Sina's a priori arguments for God's existence. [@sep-ibn-rushd, §6.1]

## Reply to al-Ghazali

[[al-ghazali]] had attacked the philosophers in [[incoherence-of-the-philosophers]]. [@sep-al-ghazali, §3] Ibn Rushd wrote [[incoherence-of-the-incoherence]] as a direct reply, at a time when al-Ghazali's work carried considerable weight in Andalusia. [@sep-ibn-rushd, §1.2] The Encyclopedia of Religion counts this refutation among the work of the western Islamic philosophers who followed the Greek tradition until about 1200. [@eor-ghazali, preamble]

He took over al-Ghazali's rule for reconciling reason with the outward sense of revelation, but he disagreed with al-Ghazali's findings on what can and cannot be demonstrated. [@sep-al-ghazali, §4] He held that Aristotle had already demonstrated the pre-eternity of the world, and he noted that no Qur'anic passage plainly states that the world was created in time. [@sep-al-ghazali, §4] Many Western readers readily accepted his denial that al-Ghazali's book was philosophy, and the Incoherence of the Philosophers was for too long read through his reply. [@sep-al-ghazali, §3] The reply was translated into Latin in 1328 and again in 1526, both times as Destructio destructionum. [@sep-al-ghazali, §4]

## Hebrew and Latin Readers

[[maimonides]] acquired his commentaries, perhaps around 1190, and his praise began four centuries of Jewish interest in Ibn Rushd. [@sep-ibn-rushd, §9.2] By 1340 nearly all the commentaries were available in Hebrew. [@sep-ibn-rushd, §9.2] Latin translation began with Michael Scott around 1220, and by the 1230s Latin authors called him "the Commentator." [@sep-ibn-rushd, §9.3] In 1270 and 1277 lists of condemned propositions at the University of Paris targeted many views associated with him. [@sep-ibn-rushd, §9.3]
