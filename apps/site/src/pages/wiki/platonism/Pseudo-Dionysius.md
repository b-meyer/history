# Pseudo-Dionysius

Pseudo-Dionysius is the name now given to a Christian [[neoplatonists|Neoplatonist]] whose writing dates to the late fifth or early sixth century [@sep-pseudo-dionysius-areopagite, preamble]. He took up pagan Neoplatonism from [[plotinus]] to [[proclus]], above all Proclus and the Platonic school at [[athens]], and recast it for a Christian setting [@sep-pseudo-dionysius-areopagite, preamble].

## The Persona

His works are written as if by Dionysius the Areopagite, a first-century member of the Athenian council of the Areopagus who was converted by [[paul]] [@sep-pseudo-dionysius-areopagite, §1]. The Stanford article says they might be regarded as a successful "forgery" that gave their author Christian credentials older than Plotinus by close to two centuries [@sep-pseudo-dionysius-areopagite, §1]. The same article points out that forgery is a modern idea, and that taking on the persona of an ancient figure was an old rhetorical device, used by others in the author's circle with names from the New Testament [@sep-pseudo-dionysius-areopagite, §1].

The author presents his teaching as received from one Hierotheus and addressed to one Timothy [@sep-pseudo-dionysius-areopagite, §1]. In the persona's own voice, On the Divine Names opens with the salutation "To my Fellow Presbyter, Timothy, Dionysius the Presbyter" [@pseudo-dionysius-works-parker, Chapter_1]. Later in the treatise he calls Hierotheus both teacher and friend, and says he was taught by him after Paul [@pseudo-dionysius-works-parker, DN 3.2]. The Stanford article describes Hierotheus as a possibly fictional teacher [@sep-pseudo-dionysius-areopagite, §3.2].

The disguise held for centuries and gave the writings near-apostolic authority in the Middle Ages and the Renaissance [@sep-pseudo-dionysius-areopagite, §1]. Hypatius of Ephesus doubted it in the sixth century, Lorenzo Valla questioned it seriously in 1457 and John Grocyn in 1501, and Erasmus publicized the critical view from 1504 [@sep-pseudo-dionysius-areopagite, §1]. The Stanford article on Proclus says the claim to be a disciple of Saint Paul was generally accepted until the late nineteenth century [@sep-proclus, §4].

## Date and Teacher

Proclus died in 485, and Severus of Antioch first clearly cites the Dionysian works between 518 and 528, so the Stanford article places their writing between those dates [@sep-pseudo-dionysius-areopagite, §1]. The corpus fits that range, since it knows the Athenian Neoplatonism of the time, the creed and liturgy of the late fifth century, and the Neoplatonic rites of theurgy [@sep-pseudo-dionysius-areopagite, §1]. The article on Proclus puts him at work around 500 [@sep-proclus, §4], and the article on Neoplatonism dates the writings to the early sixth century [@sep-neoplatonism, §8].

Modern scholars place him in Proclus's lifetime, and the Stanford article judges that he was "most probably" a pupil of Proclus, perhaps of Syrian origin [@sep-pseudo-dionysius-areopagite, §1].

## Writings

Four treatises and ten letters survive, and the author names at least seven other works that are lost or were never written [@sep-pseudo-dionysius-areopagite, §2]. The treatises are On the Divine Names, the Celestial Hierarchy, the Ecclesiastical Hierarchy and the Mystical Theology [@sep-pseudo-dionysius-areopagite, §2].

[[divine-names]] is his second theological treatise and deals with the names given to the godhead as a whole [@sep-pseudo-dionysius-areopagite, §3.2]. Each of these names carries the prefix "over-" (hyper- in Greek), giving forms such as "over-good" and "over-being" [@sep-pseudo-dionysius-areopagite, §3.2]. At its opening the author says that nothing may be said or even thought about the hidden Deity beyond what the sacred Oracles reveal [@pseudo-dionysius-works-parker, DN 1.1].

The Mystical Theology turns from affirmation to negation [@sep-pseudo-dionysius-areopagite, §3.4]. Every divine name is to be denied, and the denials are themselves denied, since the godhead lies beyond both the living and the lifeless [@sep-pseudo-dionysius-areopagite, §3.4]. The treatise opens with a prayer to the Triad and urges Timothy to leave behind sense and intellect and rise "unknowingly" to union with what is above all being and knowledge [@pseudo-dionysius-works-parker, MT 1.1]. It then tells him to affirm every attribute of things to the Cause and, more properly, to deny them all, without treating the denials as the opposite of the affirmations [@pseudo-dionysius-works-parker, MT 1.2]. In the treatise's account of Moses on Sinai, Moses passes through sensible and intelligible contemplation and then enters the darkness above the peak, where he is united with the unknowable godhead [@sep-pseudo-dionysius-areopagite, §3.4].

## Debt to Proclus

The Stanford article on Proclus says Dionysius was deeply taken with Proclus and used his works to the full in building his own Christian Platonic theology [@sep-proclus, §4]. Through him, Proclus's theology reached the Middle Ages with an almost apostolic authority, which Dodds summed up as Proclus "conquering Europe in the guise of an early Christian" [@sep-proclus, §4]. The theory of evil as privation and non-being in On the Divine Names depends on one of Proclus's short treatises [@sep-pseudo-dionysius-areopagite, §4.1]. Luther objected that Dionysius "platonizes more than he Christianizes" [@sep-pseudo-dionysius-areopagite, §4.1].

## Influence

The same theory of evil, which he took from Plotinus with changes and from Proclus, later shaped Aquinas and Ficino [@sep-pseudo-dionysius-areopagite, §5]. His sense of the beauty of the visible world inspired Abbot Suger's plan for the Gothic cathedral [@sep-pseudo-dionysius-areopagite, §5].

In the Latin West, study of his works began in earnest after the Byzantine emperor Michael the Stammerer sent a copy of the corpus to the Frankish king Louis the Pious in 827 [@sep-pseudo-dionysius-areopagite, §5]. Hilduin made the first Latin translation around 838, and John Scottus Eriugena made a new one, finished in 862 and revised in 875 [@sep-pseudo-dionysius-areopagite, §5]. The article on Neoplatonism names the Dionysian writings, beside Augustine and the Cappadocian Fathers, as a route by which Neoplatonism reached Christian theology [@sep-neoplatonism, §8].
